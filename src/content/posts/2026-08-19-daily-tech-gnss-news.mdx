Wednesday's market kept circling one question: what is the AI buildout actually worth to the companies that supply it? Google answered by taking an option on a chipmaker's stock; SpaceX answered by trying to buy its way into AI software; and OpenAI, the sector's bellwether, quietly reminded everyone that even torrid growth can disappoint when expectations are set for perfection. China, meanwhile, minted its first publicly traded humanoid-robot bet. In positioning, the news leaned toward the pointy end of PNT—systems designed to deny an adversary's navigation, and antennas small enough to put precision anywhere.

## Tech News

### Marvell Hands Google a $12.2 Billion Stock Option in Custom AI-Chip Pact

**Marvell Technology** has expanded its collaboration with **Google** to design custom silicon for the search giant's **Tensor Processing Unit (TPU)** ecosystem, and sweetened the relationship with an unusual kicker: warrants letting Google buy up to **$12.2 billion** of Marvell stock at **$206.58** a share, per Bloomberg and CNBC. That would make Google roughly Marvell's fifth-largest shareholder. Marvell shares jumped as much as **10%** on the news.

The structure ties the two companies together for the long haul. Google gets the right to almost **59 million** shares, with a small first tranche vesting up front and the rest unlocking in increments pegged to every **$500 million** of chips Google buys, per the coverage—an incentive that could translate to roughly **$120 billion** in revenue through fiscal 2033 if Google hits its purchase targets. Marvell will build a range of parts around the TPU: AI inference accelerators, networking chips, storage controllers, memory-interface controllers, and near-memory computing. As the largest cloud players increasingly design their own accelerators to lessen dependence on Nvidia, this is a vote of confidence that the merchant-silicon partners enabling that shift can capture a durable slice of the spend.

### SpaceX Sounded Out Cognition—Its Second AI Grab in Weeks

**SpaceX** approached AI coding startup **Cognition** about an acquisition, per Bloomberg, in what would have been Elon Musk's second sizable AI takeover in a matter of weeks. Cognition—best known for the autonomous software-engineering agent Devin—didn't engage with the approach, and the talks have shifted toward a possible arrangement to let Cognition run on SpaceX's computing capacity instead.

The overture is striking for who's making it. SpaceX only just completed a **$60 billion** acquisition of AI coding startup **Cursor**, per Bloomberg, and moving on Cognition days later signals how aggressively Musk is trying to consolidate AI software talent and tooling under his umbrella alongside xAI. That a rocket company is now one of the more acquisitive buyers in AI coding underscores how thoroughly the race has spilled beyond the traditional model labs—and how compute, the currency SpaceX can offer even when a deal falls through, has become the ultimate bargaining chip.

### OpenAI's Growth Underwhelms, and the Chip Slide Continues

**OpenAI** posted second-quarter revenue of about **$6.7 billion**, up **18%** from the prior quarter—solid in isolation, but soft enough against sky-high expectations to spook investors, per Yahoo Finance and reporting picked up across the tape. Semiconductor stocks extended their slide for a second session after a roughly **5%** drop the day before, as the market read a slowdown at the sector's demand engine as a warning about the durability of AI spending.

The contrast with its closest rival sharpened the anxiety. **Anthropic** recently disclosed preliminary quarterly revenue of **$11.5 billion**—a roughly 14-fold jump from a year earlier—and, per the Financial Times as cited in the coverage, its investors are now targeting a **$2 trillion** valuation in an IPO expected as soon as September or October. With the two leading labs pointed in opposite directions on momentum, the episode captures the market's central AI worry in miniature: the spending is enormous and rising, but the revenue underneath it has to keep inflecting to justify the bill.

## GNSS News

### The Pentagon Puts a Maritime 'Hammer of the Gods' to Sea to Deny Enemy PNT

Most of this year's positioning news has been about defending signals; this one is about attacking them. The **U.S. Army**, **Navy**, and **Space Force** jointly tested a maritime version of the **Hammer of the Gods** electronic-warfare system for the first time at sea, off Southern California in mid-July, during **Project Convergence–Capstone 6**, the Army's largest emerging-technology experiment, per Inside GNSS and Army coverage via DVIDS. The emitter is a highly deployable, low-electromagnetic-signature platform designed to deliver multi-domain effects at the forward edge—and, pointedly, to **disrupt the positioning, navigation, and timing data that enemy precision weapons rely on**.

The maritime debut matters because it extends a land-proven capability into the naval domain, where denying an adversary's GNSS could blunt guided munitions before they reach a target. Land-based versions have appeared in exercises over the past two years; putting the PNT-focused variant on the water folds electronic attack into the joint force's maritime playbook. It's the flip side of the resilience story that has dominated 2026: the same jamming and spoofing threats civil aviation has been sounding alarms over are, in the military's hands, a deliberate weapon—and the U.S. is now demonstrating it can wield that weapon from the sea.

### Taoglas Squeezes Dual-Band Precision Into a 20 mm Patch

On the commercial side, the trend is high precision in ever-smaller packages. **Taoglas** introduced its **GVLB208** series, an active-and-passive dual-band GNSS **L1/L5** stacked-patch antenna in a **20 x 20 x 8 mm** footprint, recently highlighted by Inside GNSS and GPS World. Using a single-feed stacked-patch design, it supports concurrent L1 and L5 reception across GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou, with peak gain up to **1.5 dBi**, roughly **50%** efficiency across both bands, and stable right-hand circular polarization—enough for reliable centimeter-level positioning.

The point is where that performance can now go. Concurrent L1/L5 is what unlocks faster convergence and better multipath rejection for high-precision positioning, and shrinking it to a 20 mm patch makes it viable for drones, telematics, fleet and asset tracking, precision agriculture, and industrial IoT—applications where board space and weight are tight. Taoglas offers passive (**GVLB208.A**) and active (**AGVLB208.A**) variants and plans an SMD version tuned for high-volume automated manufacturing later this year. As dual-frequency correction services spread, antennas like this are the quiet enablers that let centimeter accuracy scale into mass-market hardware.

## Key Takeaways

- **The AI arms race is being priced in equity, not just cash**: Marvell gave Google a warrant to buy up to $12.2 billion of its stock in a custom-silicon deal worth a potential $120 billion through fiscal 2033, while SpaceX—fresh off a $60 billion Cursor acquisition—sounded out Cognition, showing how deep-pocketed buyers are locking up chips and coding talent with structures far beyond ordinary M&A.
- **Even the leaders have to keep inflecting**: OpenAI's 18% sequential growth to $6.7 billion disappointed and extended a two-day chip-stock slide, even as Anthropic's $11.5 billion quarter and reported $2 trillion IPO target showed the market will still pay up for momentum—capturing the sector's core tension between colossal spending and the revenue needed to justify it.
- **PNT is now offense as well as defense**: The Army, Navy, and Space Force tested a maritime Hammer of the Gods emitter built to deny enemy positioning and navigation, while Taoglas packed dual-band L1/L5 precision into a 20 mm antenna—resilient positioning being weaponized on one end and miniaturized for mass-market autonomy on the other.
