For two weeks the market had treated every dollar of AI capital spending as a liability, selling the hyperscalers for the cash their data centers consume. On Tuesday—Election Day—the reflex reversed. Chip stocks rebounded through the regular session, and then the bell rang on a run of AI-infrastructure earnings so strong that investors, for once, bought them. The Dow was the lone holdout, pulled lower by a cautious Apple margin outlook, while rising bond yields hinted the macro backdrop is not yet all-clear. In positioning, the month's resilience thread carried on: a compact inertial box built to keep ships navigating when satellites go dark, and a national timing authority asking what its oldest radio signal is still good for.

## Tech News

### AMD's Record Quarter Leads an AI-Chip Rebound

The night's defining number came from the chipmaker that had spent the prior two weeks as collateral damage. After the close, **AMD** reported record third-quarter revenue of **$13.4 billion**, up **45%** and above the top of its own forecast, with **data-center sales surging 97% to $8.5 billion** as its AI accelerators and server processors caught the buildout wave, per StockTitan. Earnings came in at **$2.09** a share against a **$1.94** estimate, and management guided fourth-quarter revenue to roughly **$17 billion**, ahead of the **$16.23 billion** consensus, per cappnotes.

Investors rewarded it rather than flinching at it. AMD rose about **7%** in after-hours trading on top of a **3.65%** regular-session gain—a sharp turn from the stretch when the same stock had been dragged down in sympathy with every capex-spooked selloff. The report reframed the question the market had been asking all of October: here was AI demand showing up as record revenue and accelerating margins, not just as an invoice.

### The Market Snaps Its Losing Streak as Chips Rebound

The regular session had already set the stage. The **Nasdaq Composite** climbed **0.83%** to **25,372.01** and the **S&P 500** added **0.36%** to **7,568.92**, snapping a three-day losing streak, while the **Russell 2000** edged up **0.23%** to **2,812.45**, per cappnotes. The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** was the exception, falling **0.38%** (**-197.26**) to **51,786.72** as **Apple** slid **1.90%** on a weak margin forecast.

Semiconductors did the heavy lifting: the **PHLX Semiconductor Index** jumped **2.63%**, with **Broadcom** up **3.07%**, **Micron** up **2.58%**, and **Nvidia** up **2.29%**. Technology (**+1.31%**) and communication services (**+1.18%**) led the sectors, while materials (**-2.01%**) and consumer staples (**-2.07%**) lagged in a session that leaned back toward the AI names. The one caution came from rates: strong **JOLTS** job-openings data pushed the **10-year Treasury yield** up four basis points to **4.95%**, a reminder that the Fed backdrop still argues against a clean risk-on turn.

### Arista and Super Micro Extend the AI-Hardware Beats After the Bell

AMD was not alone after hours. **Arista Networks**, which sells the high-speed switching that stitches AI clusters together, leapt **14.19%** in extended trading after reporting revenue of **$3.66 billion**—up **58.5%** and well above its own **$3.33 billion** guide—with earnings of **$1.20** a share against a **$1.08** estimate, per cappnotes. **Super Micro Computer**, the AI-server assembler, rose about **8%** on record quarterly sales of **$19.9 billion** and raised guidance.

The common thread was telling. The companies selling the picks and shovels of the AI buildout—accelerators, networking, and servers—all posted the end-customer demand that the hyperscalers' enormous capital budgets had implied, and investors treated those numbers as validation rather than warning. After a fortnight in which the market punished the spenders, Tuesday night rewarded the suppliers.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Skyworks slides on soft guidance**: **Skyworks Solutions** fell about **15%** after hours after guiding December-quarter revenue to just **$1.80 billion to $1.90 billion** with EPS near **$1.25**, a reminder that the broader chip tape is still splitting winners from laggards.
- **Duolingo jumps on user growth**: **Duolingo** rose **12.29%** in extended trading as daily active users grew **29%** year over year.
- **Amdocs craters on a weak outlook**: **Amdocs** plunged more than **43%** after hours after forecasting fiscal 2027 revenue to fall between **14.3%** and **17.9%**.
- **Nurix tumbles on a pipeline setback**: **Nurix Therapeutics** dropped about **34%** after **Sanofi** discontinued a drug candidate from the two companies' partnership.
- **Yields climb on strong labor data**: Robust JOLTS job openings lifted Treasury yields across the curve, with the 10-year reaching **4.95%** and the 30-year near **5.32%**.

## GNSS News

### Exail Launches the Compact Marins M1 INS for GNSS-Denied Navigation

The freshest positioning story was a box built for the moment satellites disappear. On **October 26**, French navigation specialist **Exail** launched the **Marins M1**, a compact inertial navigation system (INS) that brings "high-performance, resilient navigation" to a wider range of naval platforms, per GPS World. Built on the company's sea-proven **Fiber Optic Gyroscope (FOG)** technology, the M1 targets rigid-hulled inflatable boats, fast interception and patrol craft, and uncrewed surface and underwater vehicles—platforms where size, integration, and cost have kept high-grade inertial navigation out of reach.

The headline specification speaks directly to a jamming-and-spoofing threat environment: the Marins M1 holds a maximum position drift of **one nautical mile after three hours** with **no external aiding**, while continuously outputting position, heading, roll, and pitch. By pairing that drift performance with what Exail calls a "more accessible price point," the launch aims to push autonomous and crewed vessels toward carrying a self-contained fallback that keeps working when GNSS is degraded or denied—exactly the gap the past month's resilience stories have circled.

### The UK Opens a Consultation on the Future of Its National Time Signal

The complementary story came from the policy side and looked at the humblest piece of timing infrastructure. On **November 2**, GPS World reported that the UK's **National PNT Office (NPNT)** is considering changes to public use of the **60 kHz MSF signal**—the country's legacy long-wave radio time and frequency service, receivable across much of the UK and traceable to the **National Physical Laboratory's** time scale. Rather than announce a decision, the office is asking users how the signal is actually relied upon: how critical it is, what future requirements exist, what unique capabilities it provides, and what barriers stand in the way of wider adoption.

The context is institutional. The NPNT was established within the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology as part of the government's **Framework for Greater PNT Resilience**, with a remit spanning PNT policy, coordination, and delivery across Critical National Infrastructure and the wider economy. It is seeking input from national infrastructure operators, industrial and commercial users, researchers, and individual consumers alike—a sign that even a decades-old terrestrial broadcast is being re-examined for its place in a layered defense against GNSS disruption.
