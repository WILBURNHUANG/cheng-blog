Thursday's news kept coming back to a single move: reaching past the product everyone already depends on to control the layer sitting on top of it. Nvidia, which owns the chips, bought the platform where AI models actually live. Google shipped a cheap, fast model to everyone and reserved its most dangerous cyber capabilities for a trusted few. And a Chinese frontier lab reached for the public markets to fund its own climb. In positioning, the throughline was fragility underneath the signal: a national satellite system quietly demoted to a supporting role, and a commercial LEO alternative proving it can hold when GPS is being faked.

## Tech News

### Nvidia Buys Hugging Face for $12.93 Billion in Its Biggest Deal Ever

**Nvidia** agreed to acquire **Hugging Face** for **$12.93 billion**, the largest acquisition in the chipmaker's history, giving it control of the platform that hosts more than **3 million** AI models, roughly **500,000** datasets, and over **1 million** applications, used by a community of more than **18 million** developers and **200,000** companies, per the Financial Times via Tech Startups. CEO **Jensen Huang** said Hugging Face will keep operating as an open platform and that developers will not be required to use Nvidia hardware.

The deal pushes Nvidia up the stack from silicon into the software layer where models are discovered, fine-tuned, evaluated, and deployed. That matters precisely because so many of Nvidia's biggest customers are now designing their own accelerators to lean less on its GPUs—owning the developer marketplace keeps Nvidia central even as the hardware fragments. It also raises an obvious antitrust question: one company would control both the dominant AI compute and one of the industry's most important model hubs, and keeping Hugging Face genuinely hardware-neutral will be the test everyone watches.

### Google Ships Gemini 3.8 Flash and Locks Its Cyber Model Behind Vetted Access

**Google DeepMind** released **Gemini 3.8 Flash** alongside a security-focused sibling, **Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber**, and handled the two very differently. The standard model improves software engineering, long-running agent tasks, and multi-step reasoning while holding introductory pricing at **$0.75** per million input tokens and **$3.75** per million output tokens, per Google DeepMind. Flash Cyber, which adds advanced vulnerability detection and automated patching, is being restricted through a new **Fairwind Program** open only to trusted governments, critical-infrastructure operators, and software maintainers.

The split is the real story. The same capability that lets a model find and fix security holes can automate offensive cyber operations, so Google is treating cybersecurity as a gated access tier rather than a general feature—the same pattern OpenAI used days earlier when it restricted its "Critical"-rated Astra model. Two frontier labs drawing the same line in the same week looks less like coincidence than an emerging industry template for how offensive-grade AI gets released.

### China's Moonshot AI Files for Hong Kong IPO at Roughly $50 Billion

**Moonshot AI**, the Beijing startup behind the **Kimi** family of large language models, has confidentially filed for an initial public offering in Hong Kong at a valuation around **$50 billion**, per The Wall Street Journal and Reuters. Founded in 2023 by **Yang Zhilin** and backed by **Alibaba**, **Tencent**, and **HSG**, the company would stage one of the largest AI listings yet to come out of China, following earlier public-market moves by MiniMax and Z.AI.

The filing marks how fast China's frontier-model labs are shifting from venture-funded research shops toward public capital. A successful listing would hand Moonshot a war chest for chips, training infrastructure, and talent, and give investors a fresh benchmark for valuing Chinese AI—a sharp contrast with U.S. labs like OpenAI and Anthropic that keep raising enormous sums while staying private.

## GNSS News

### India Concedes Its NavIC Constellation Can No Longer Navigate on Its Own

India's government has formally accepted that its seven-satellite **NavIC** system can no longer independently provide navigation, its first official acknowledgment since one satellite's positioning data was lost after an atomic-clock failure in March, per GPS World citing the Indian Express. The constellation can still broadcast accurate timing and emergency messages, and India's armed forces keep using NavIC as one input within a multi-constellation mix alongside **GPS**, **Galileo**, and **GLONASS**.

The admission is a candid reminder that regional GNSS systems are only as strong as their satellite health, and that redundancy across constellations—not any single system—is what keeps receivers working. ISRO says the replacement satellite **NVS-03** is ready for launch, with **NVS-04** and **NVS-05** in advanced stages, so the gap is meant to be temporary. But for now the country's home-built alternative to GPS depends on the very foreign systems it was designed to hedge against.

### Furuno Pulls Live Xona LEO Signals Through a Spoofing Test in Japan

Japanese receiver maker **Furuno** became the first company in Japan to receive live signals from **Xona Space Systems'** **Pulsar-0**, the debut satellite in Xona's next-generation LEO positioning fleet, per GPS World. In a July test at the **Fukushima Robot Test Field**, Furuno confirmed it could keep receiving Pulsar signals reliably even while spoofing disrupted traditional GNSS—using a commercially available GNSS antenna and a standard receiver with only minor firmware changes, since Pulsar transmits in the L-band near existing GNSS frequencies.

The significance is how cheap the path to resilience turned out to be. Rather than build dedicated hardware, Furuno leaned on existing receiver technology to add a satellite-based backup that is independent of GNSS but compatible with it—useful for the critical infrastructure that relies on GPS timing, from mobile networks and broadcasting to financial transactions and power grids. It follows a February MoU between the two companies and, with more Pulsar satellites due this year, moves commercial LEO-PNT from concept toward something integrators can actually field.
