The new week began where the last one left off—arguing about who is paying for the AI boom, and whether the money is moving in a circle. Nvidia answered by announcing even bigger deals, the kind that tie its fortunes ever more tightly to the customers buying its chips. Washington and Beijing, meanwhile, turned an accusation about copied models into a full trade-policy standoff, and a quieter Bloomberg data point suggested AI is already reshaping cybersecurity by finding flaws faster than humans ever could. In positioning, Europe closed out a milestone with almost no fanfare: two more Galileo satellites went operational.

## Tech News

### Nvidia's $750 Billion in New Deals Reignite the Circular-Financing Debate

**Nvidia** is working on a fresh round of AI deals worth more than **$750 billion**, a scale large enough to revive the long-running worry that the company is quietly manufacturing its own demand, per Bloomberg. The centerpiece is a partnership with South Korea's **SK Group**, unveiled late Friday, that the two sides value at more than **$500 billion**—a figure that bundles Nvidia's purchases of memory chips with SK's purchases of Nvidia supercomputers. On top of that, Nvidia is in talks to backstop as much as **$250 billion** to help **OpenAI** lease computing power from a U.S. data-center project.

The mechanics are exactly what critics keep pointing at: Nvidia sells the chips, then helps finance the buyers and infrastructure projects that consume them. Bulls read the SK tie-up as smart supply-chain defense—the arrangement improves Nvidia's access to high-bandwidth memory, and Nvidia will help SK Hynix design future HBM parts, locking in scarce supply. Skeptics counter that lending demand its own money can distort incentives across the industry and magnify the losses if AI revenue never catches up to the spending. After a week in which the market punished Big Tech for overspending, Nvidia's response was to spend more.

### China Vows to Retaliate Over U.S. Threat to Sanction Its AI Firms

**China** warned it would take "all necessary measures" if the United States follows through on a threat to sanction Chinese artificial-intelligence companies, escalating a dispute over whether Chinese labs improperly trained their models on American ones, per Bloomberg and Xinhua. Treasury Secretary **Scott Bessent** had floated sanctions and Entity List blacklisting over what U.S. officials call industrial-scale "distillation," citing forensic evidence of American model watermarks inside Chinese products. Beijing's Commerce Ministry called the accusations baseless and a "typical form of AI hegemony," and argued that distillation is a widely used technique—one, it claimed, that American firms use too.

The flashpoint is **Moonshot AI's** Kimi K3, released July 16, which U.S. officials allege was distilled from leading American models. The fight puts a policy edge on a technical practice, and it isn't one-sided at home: nearly **200 U.S. startups** have urged the government not to cut off access to Chinese open-source models, warning that doing so would blunt their own competitiveness. However the sanctions question resolves, it marks the moment the model-training arms race became an explicit trade-and-security dispute between the two governments.

### AI Is Now Finding Software Flaws Twice as Fast as Last Year

The number of software-security vulnerabilities uncovered in popular technology products in 2026 is on pace to roughly **double** the 2025 tally, an explosion Bloomberg attributes to increasingly capable AI systems turned loose on codebases. Automated tools that can read, reason about, and probe software are surfacing bugs at a rate human researchers never matched, compressing work that used to take specialized teams weeks into machine-speed sweeps.

It is a genuine double-edged result. The same capability that lets defenders find and patch flaws before they are exploited also hands attackers a faster way to discover them first, and the balance depends on who deploys it more effectively. For an industry already spending its way through an AI arms race, the finding reframes part of that spend as a security necessity rather than a growth bet—AI that hunts vulnerabilities is quickly becoming table stakes, not a differentiator.

## GNSS News

### Galileo's SAT 33 and SAT 34 Enter Service, Completing Europe's L14 Batch

Europe's **Galileo** constellation added two operational satellites as **SAT 33** and **SAT 34** entered service, with the EU's Security Accreditation Board authorizing SAT 34's activation on **July 23, 2026**—a step that completes deployment of the first-generation **L14** satellites, per ESA and Via Satellite. The pair launched in December 2025 aboard an **Ariane 6**, in what ESA called the "most precise orbital injection ever achieved for Galileo," and now contribute capacity, resilience, and autonomy to a network that European officials bill as the world's most accurate global navigation system.

The milestone is incremental by design, and that is the point. Each satellite brought online deepens the redundancy that matters most in an era of routine jamming and spoofing—more healthy signals in the sky mean more ways for a receiver to hold a fix when some are degraded or denied. Four Galileo First Generation satellites still remain to be launched, with the next pair slated for the end of the year, even as the EU turns its attention to the second-generation spacecraft meant to carry features like higher-power signals and authenticated navigation. For now, Galileo quietly got a little more robust—the unglamorous, essential work of keeping a constellation full.
