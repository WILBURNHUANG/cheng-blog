Tuesday's news put AI's two mounting bills side by side. On one side, the cost of building it: Anthropic signed yet another multi-billion-dollar compute lease, adding to a stack of deals that now dwarfs most companies' entire market caps. On the other, the cost of running it at scale: regulators moved on both an old-guard giant and a new one, with the FTC accusing Amazon of quietly gouging its advertisers and the EU deciding that a chatbot with a search box is, legally, a search engine. In positioning, the throughline was navigation that ignores satellites entirely—a quantum sensor reading Earth's gravity to cross open water, arriving just as Europe's jamming problem gets measurably worse.

## Tech News

### Anthropic Locks In Another $35 Billion of Compute, This Time With Nvidia-Backed Lambda

**Anthropic** signed a roughly **$35 billion**, six-year cloud agreement with **Lambda**, an Nvidia-backed provider, to rapidly expand the computing power behind its **Claude** models, per Bloomberg and The Information. The capacity sits in **Nueces County, Texas**, at a facility being built out by **Hut 8**—a former Bitcoin miner pivoting to data centers—with **Nvidia** retaining the lease and supplying the chips that Lambda resells to Anthropic.

The deal is striking less for its size than for how routine that size has become. It follows Anthropic's **$45 billion** lease for Nscale's West Virginia campus, a **$50 billion** arrangement with neocloud Fluidstack, and a **$45 billion** deal with SpaceX—more than **$175 billion** in committed compute from a single model developer across four providers. The structure also underscores Nvidia's grip on the whole chain: it invests in the cloud startup, owns the facility lease, and sells the silicon, capturing margin at every layer while its customer chases capacity it can't build fast enough itself.

### FTC and 22 States Sue Amazon, Alleging It Overcharged Advertisers More Than $20 Billion

The **Federal Trade Commission** and **22 state attorneys general** sued **Amazon**, alleging it "secretly and systematically overcharged" **1.2 million** advertising customers by more than **$20 billion** since 2019, per CNN and CNBC. The complaint says Amazon manipulated the auctions behind three ad products—**Sponsored Products**, **Sponsored Brands**, and **Sponsored Display**—inflating prices while giving advertisers inadequate disclosure. More than **500,000** of the affected customers were small and medium businesses.

The stakes reach past Amazon's ad unit, one of its fastest-growing and highest-margin businesses. Regulators argue the inflated ad costs ultimately fed through to higher prices for consumers, tying an advertising-practices case to a broader affordability argument. Amazon said it "strongly disagrees" with the suit and denied the allegations, setting up a fight over how much daylight sellers are owed into the black-box auctions that increasingly decide what shoppers see first.

### EU Designates ChatGPT a 'Very Large Online Search Engine,' a First for an AI Chatbot

The **European Commission** classified **ChatGPT** as a "Very Large Online Search Engine" (VLOSE) under the **Digital Services Act**—the first time an AI assistant has been regulated as a search engine, per Business Standard and Gizmodo. The trigger was scale: **OpenAI** reported roughly **159.1 million** average monthly EU users, more than triple the **45 million** threshold. The classification turns specifically on ChatGPT's live web-search function, not its status as an AI product.

That capability-based logic is the real news. By hanging the designation on the search box rather than the model, Brussels created a template it can extend to **Gemini**, **Claude**, and **Perplexity** as they scale. OpenAI now has four months—until **January 2027**—to comply with the heaviest DSA obligations: annual systemic-risk assessments, a public ad repository, vetted-researcher data access, twice-yearly transparency reports, and a crisis-response mechanism.

## GNSS News

### Q-CTRL Steers a Ship Across the Coral Sea With No GPS, Using a Quantum Gravimeter

The clearest "navigate without satellites" story of the week came from the water. Australian quantum-software firm **Q-CTRL** demonstrated what it called a world-first: GPS-free maritime navigation using a software-ruggedized **quantum gravimeter**, guiding a **29-meter** vessel through the **Coral Sea** off Australia's east coast, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. Its **Ironstone Opal** platform read the faint "hills and valleys" of Earth's gravity field against onboard maps to hold **1-nautical-mile** positioning accuracy over an **83-km** trajectory—beating a navigation-grade inertial backup by more than **10x**.

The significance is that gravity can't be jammed or spoofed. Where much of 2026's resilience work layers new radio signals to survive interference, gravimetric navigation sidesteps the electromagnetic spectrum entirely, reading a physical field no adversary can fake. Q-CTRL framed the trial against a hostile backdrop: roughly **978,000** GPS jamming events were logged globally in Q1 2026, **98%** of them in the Middle East, affecting more than **1,100** vessels. A sensor that positions off bedrock rather than orbit is exactly the kind of last-resort layer navies have been asking for.

### Poland Reports GNSS Jamming on Nearly Two-Thirds of Days Along Its Baltic Coast

The demand for that resilience was underscored by fresh data from Poland. The country's **National Institute of Telecommunications** reported significant GNSS interference across all frequency bands on **63%** of days during the first 19 days of August at its **Gdańsk** monitoring station, covering the **Tri-City** area of Gdańsk, Gdynia, and Sopot, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The disruption ranges from single-band degradation to multi-hour, full-spectrum blackouts, and has climbed steadily since the start of 2026—earlier in the year, May and June saw all-band outages on as many as **70%** of days.

The fallout is civilian as much as military: knocked-out receivers hit shared transport, municipal fleets, and commercial drones across the region. Poland's Civil Aviation Authority has told **ICAO** that Baltic interference originates from Russian territory, and researchers have traced it to electronic-warfare sites near Kaliningrad. The report reframes GNSS jamming as a chronic operating condition rather than an occasional incident—one reason alternatives like Q-CTRL's gravimeter are moving from novelty to necessity.
