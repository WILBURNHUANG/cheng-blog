Wednesday's news kept circling two questions: how much authority are we willing to hand to AI, and what does it take to keep it running? Meta wants an agent holding the keys to your email and payments. Google decided the scarce resource worth locking up for 22 years is electricity, not silicon. OpenAI claimed a machine solved one of math's hardest open problems—and immediately had to defend how it got there. In positioning, the throughline was the opposite instinct: trust nothing overhead. A ship found its way across open water by feeling the shape of Earth's gravity, and the U.S. Navy kept investing in the machines that make everyone else's satellites useless.

## Tech News

### Meta Launches Muse, a Personal AI Agent That Can Email, Shop, and Book Travel

**Meta** launched **Muse**, a personal AI agent that goes well past answering questions—it can manage a calendar, draft and send emails, book travel, compare prices, shop, and fill out forms across connected apps, rolling out to U.S. adults, per WIRED. Built on Meta's **Muse Spark** models, the agent runs inside a dedicated secure virtual machine with its own browser and hooks into Google Workspace, Ticketmaster, OpenTable, Spotify, and Apple Health, with purchases handled through **Stripe's Link** checkout. It's reachable through a standalone app, the web, or WhatsApp, with a free tier alongside **$20** and **$100** monthly plans.

It's the most concrete product yet from Mark Zuckerberg's "personal superintelligence" push, and an attempt to turn more than **$130 billion** in planned 2026 AI infrastructure spending into a business beyond ads. The catch is trust: Reuters reported internal concern that the agent can mishandle sensitive personal data, and the launch lands less than two weeks after Meta's **$18 billion** multistate settlement over social-media harms. Chatbots competed on the quality of their answers; agents will compete on how much real-world authority users will actually delegate—putting Meta head to head with OpenAI and Google for the layer between people and their inboxes, wallets, and calendars.

### Google Commits $15 Billion to Finland and Locks In 22 Years of Nuclear Power

**Google** is making its largest single investment in Europe, committing at least **€13 billion (about $15.1 billion)** to AI infrastructure and energy projects in Finland across 2027 and 2028, covering new data-center capacity at four locations including its long-running Hamina campus, per Reuters. The unusual part is the energy deal: Google signed a **22-year** agreement with Finnish utility **Fortum** to buy up to **half** the electricity generated by the **Loviisa** nuclear plant between 2030 and 2049.

The move shows the AI buildout pushing past GPUs and server racks into national electricity systems. Finland offers abundant low-carbon generation, a cool climate that cuts cooling costs, and proximity to European customers, and Google is also backing grid upgrades, wind, and batteries. Nuclear plants that once looked like legacy infrastructure are becoming strategic assets for the AI economy—and the next phase of the race may be constrained as much by reliable power as by access to advanced chips.

### OpenAI Says AI Solved the 90-Year-Old Navier–Stokes Problem—Then Faces a Credit Fight

**OpenAI** says an internal system well beyond GPT-6 Astra produced a solution to the **Navier–Stokes** Millennium Prize Problem, one of math's most famous open questions, per TechStartups. By OpenAI's account, roughly **10,000** coordinating AI agents worked for **88 hours** before producing an analytical proof that a three-dimensional fluid governed by the equations can develop a singularity in finite time, along with a formalized proof in **Lean**—though independent mathematicians will ultimately decide whether it holds.

The claim arrived with a dispute over attribution. NYU mathematician **Tristan Buckmaster** and Anthropic researcher **Levent Alpöge** had been pursuing related work, and Buckmaster questioned whether OpenAI accelerated its effort after learning of their progress; OpenAI denies its researchers or models improperly accessed the work. Whatever the controversy reveals, the technological story is hard to ignore: frontier systems are moving from helping scientists with calculations toward coordinating large populations of agents to attack research problems directly. If the result is independently validated, it would be unusually strong evidence that AI agent swarms can contribute at the frontier of mathematics.

## GNSS News

### Q-CTRL Steers a Ship 83 km Across the Coral Sea on Gravity Alone

The sharpest "lose the satellite, keep the mission" story came from open water. Quantum firm **Q-CTRL** completed its first open-water field demonstration of a GPS-free, quantum-gravimetric maritime navigation system, running its **Ironstone Opal** platform aboard a 29-meter vessel in the **Coral Sea** off Australia, per GPS World. The system held **bounded 1-nautical-mile** positioning accuracy with no satellite signals across an **83-km** trajectory—beating standard navigation-grade inertial backups by more than **10x**.

Instead of listening for a signal, gravity-aided navigation ("GravNav") feels the terrain: the hybrid sensor pairs cold-atom interferometry with classical accelerometers, using the atomic state as an absolute reference to cut long-term drift by about **70x**, then cross-references locally measured gravity anomalies against broad satellite-derived gravity maps to keep position error bounded. Q-CTRL cited more than **978,000** GPS jamming and spoofing incidents recorded globally in the first quarter of 2026—and real cases of tankers running aground after silent spoofing—as the reason it matters, positioning GravNav as both an unjammable alternative and an early-warning tripwire for compromised satellite data. Notably, the AI-stabilized sensor rode in an unconditioned passenger cabin without heavy motion-stabilization rigs, and the company is working with DARPA, the U.S. Defense Innovation Unit, and the Australian and UK defense establishments.

### Marotta Controls Joins the U.S. Navy's Next-Generation Airborne Jammer

While much of the week's GNSS news was about defending signals, this one is about denying them. **Marotta Controls** won two contracts supporting the U.S. Navy's **Next Generation Jammer – Low Band (NGJ-LB)** engineering and manufacturing development program, marking the company's entry into the airborne electronic-warfare market, per GPS World. The work—a ram-air-turbine power-generation system, via a subcontract with **CFD Research** and a direct award from prime contractor **L3Harris**—feeds the pod that powers the jammer.

NGJ-LB is part of the broader NGJ system that will augment and eventually replace the legacy **AN/ALQ-99** on the **EA-18G Growler**, using active electronically scanned array technology to disrupt, deny, and degrade enemy air defense and communications. The Navy awarded L3Harris a **$587.4 million** development contract for NGJ-LB in August 2024, with operational prototype pods due to Naval Air Systems Command for fleet assessment over the next five years. It's a reminder that the same electronic-warfare arms race driving all the resilience work has an offensive side—and it's a growth market with real budget behind it.
