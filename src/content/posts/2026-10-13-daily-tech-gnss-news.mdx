Monday's news kept returning to two linked questions: who gets to build the silicon behind the AI boom, and who can actually afford to buy it. The clearest answer to the first came from the biggest cloud buyer unveiling its own third-generation accelerator to lean off Nvidia, with a chip rival open-sourcing a competing rack design the same day. The second question got a blunt reply from a conference stage in Singapore, where two chip-industry CEOs said capital—not wafers, memory, or power—is now the thing most likely to throttle the buildout. In positioning, the steady lesson held once more: harden against a lost signal, whether by manufacturing GPS-denied navigation gear on home soil or by stacking a new positioning layer in low Earth orbit.

## Tech News

### Microsoft Unveils the Maia 300, Its Bid to Lean Off Nvidia

The day's defining hardware story was the largest AI cloud buyer showing it intends to make more of its own silicon. **Microsoft** announced the **Maia 300**, its third-generation custom accelerator, built on **TSMC's N2P** (2-nanometer) process with **288GB** of **HBM4** memory and **18 TB/s** of bandwidth, per Reuters reporting by Stephen Nellis and Max Cherney. Microsoft claims the chip delivers about **40% more** tokens per megawatt than leading GPU-based systems and up to **7x** the rack-scale throughput of its Maia 200, and says it plans to deploy potentially **more than one million** of them next year.

The significance is in the design choice and the scale behind it. Rather than copy Nvidia's single large compute die ringed by memory, Microsoft stitches together chiplets—reserving the expensive 2nm process for compute and cheaper 3nm for moving data—and packs racks of **96** chips drawing about **167 kilowatts**, against the roughly **142 kilowatts** of Nvidia's 72-GPU GB300 racks. Multi-gigawatt deployments start in **2027**, against capital spending Microsoft expects to exceed **$175 billion** this year. "The design delivers more intelligence for every watt, dollar, and byte of memory," said Rani Borkar, president of Azure hardware systems. After a year in which Nvidia's accelerators were the one thing every hyperscaler had to buy, the Maia 300 is Microsoft's clearest statement yet that it would rather build than keep paying the toll.

### Broadcom and Intel CEOs Say the Real AI Bottleneck Is Capital, Not Chips

The most telling read on where the buildout gets stuck came from two chip chiefs on the same stage. Speaking Monday at the **DBS Asia Future Forward Conference** in Singapore, **Broadcom** CEO **Hock Tan** and **Intel** CEO **Lip-Bu Tan** both argued that financing, not supply, is now the binding constraint, per CNBC. "What I think might be the biggest constraint is capital," Hock Tan said, suggesting wafer shortages may ease by **2028 or 2029** even as computing demand outruns supply through the end of the decade.

Intel's Lip-Bu Tan was blunter about the other half of the problem: "If there's any challenge in the front, it's capital and the cost of capital," adding that "people want to see the ROI." The remarks matter because they come from the companies selling into the boom, not skeptics watching from outside—and they land as **Broadcom** is reportedly seeking more than **$50 billion** from investors including **Apollo** and **Blackstone** to fund custom AI chips for **OpenAI**, a deal expected to close by year-end. After a month of record debt raises, the people building the picks and shovels are now saying out loud that the money, and the question of whether it will ever earn a return, is the thing to watch.

### AMD Open-Sources a Rival Rack Design for Agentic AI

The competitive counterpoint to Microsoft's in-house chip was a rival making its blueprint public. **AMD** introduced **Helion**, an open, **OCP**-based rack-scale reference design aimed at agentic AI workloads, per Tech Startups. A CPU-only configuration built on sixth-generation **EPYC "Venice"** processors supports up to **32,768** Zen 6 cores per rack, while an all-in-one variant pairs those EPYC CPUs with **Instinct MI350P** GPUs; partner configurations are expected in late **2026** or early **2027**.

The move matters because it attacks the same lock-in Microsoft's Maia is designed to escape, but from the opposite direction. Where Microsoft keeps its silicon proprietary, AMD is betting that publishing an open rack design—one anyone can build to—will pull more of the market toward its CPUs and GPUs as a credible alternative to buying whole Nvidia systems. Both are responses to the same pressure: with capital now the scarce input, every buyer is looking for a way to get more compute per dollar, and the vendors are racing to offer it.

## GNSS News

### Advanced Navigation Opens a US Hub to Build GPS-Denied Navigation Gear

The freshest positioning news was about making resilient navigation on home soil. **Advanced Navigation America**, the US arm of the Sydney-based firm, is establishing a **Positioning, Navigation and Timing (PNT) Center of Excellence** in **Huntsville, Alabama**, to research, develop, and manufacture navigation technology for GPS-denied environments, per Inside GNSS. The site—backed by the company's **$110 million** Series C from March—will build counter-electromagnetic-warfare technology spanning fiber optics, MEMS, laser sensing, photonics, vision-based systems, AI, and quantum sensing, adding a US manufacturing node alongside the company's Australian and UK operations.

The move matters because assured positioning is only useful if it can be produced at scale for the customers who need it. Advanced Navigation's core products are strategic-grade **fiber optic gyroscopes (FOGs)** and laser velocity sensors—inertial systems that keep vehicles and weapons oriented when satellite signals are jammed or absent—and it says more than **100,000** systems are already deployed worldwide, with customers including Anduril, Kongsberg, Rheinmetall, BAE Systems, Leonardo, and Lockheed Martin, plus US Navy and Army work through the All-Domain Persistent Experiment. "Assured PNT is becoming the backbone of modern autonomy," said Mike Cohen, president of Advanced Navigation America. Building that backbone domestically adds both supply-chain redundancy and production capacity for US defense programs that cannot rely on GPS alone.

### ESA's Celeste Pathfinder Confirms Positioning From Low Earth Orbit

The complementary story was about adding an entirely new layer to the positioning stack. Early in-orbit results from **ESA's Celeste** pathfinder mission confirm that a **Septentrio** receiver prototype can acquire and track dual-frequency ranging signals broadcast from **low Earth orbit**, per Inside GNSS, with findings presented by Sibren De Bast at **ION GNSS+ 2026** in Orlando. The two demonstration satellites—**IOD-1** (GMV) and **IOD-2** (TAS)—orbit at about **510 km** and transmit on **E5** (L-band) and **SR** (S-band) after launching in March 2026 from Rocket Lab's New Zealand complex.

The significance is what LEO adds that today's medium-Earth-orbit constellations cannot. In a first live pass on **June 10**, a rooftop antenna at Septentrio's Leuven headquarters tracked an IOD-1 satellite to a peak carrier-to-noise ratio near **62 dB-Hz**, and the measured ionospheric delay on the S-band signal was roughly **4.4 times smaller** than on E5—both benefits of broadcasting from a few hundred kilometers up rather than 20,000. ESA frames LEO PNT not as a replacement for GPS and Galileo but as an added layer atop the MEO backbone, part of building a resilient, multi-layer positioning system that is far harder to jam or spoof than any single constellation on its own.
