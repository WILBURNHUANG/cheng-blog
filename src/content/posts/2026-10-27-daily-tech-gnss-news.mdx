The AI trade snapped back to open the week, reversing the spending-anxiety mood that had knocked tech lower for two straight weeks. What turned the tape was not a change in the debate about whether the buildout will pay off, but fresh evidence of how much capital is still flooding into it: the sector's best-funded private lab filed to go public, one rocket company borrowed tens of billions to buy chips, and a social-media giant lined up a stock sale to match. Underneath sat a wall of catalysts—a Federal Reserve meeting and the quarter's heaviest earnings week both land in the next few days. In positioning, the month's resilience theme kept moving from the lab toward the shelf: navigation that does not depend on vulnerable GPS signals showed up as a mass-market-ready antenna and a coin-sized board for autonomous systems.

## Tech News

### Anthropic Files to Go Public, Reigniting the AI Trade

The week's defining story was a prospectus. **Anthropic** publicly filed its **S-1** with the SEC on Monday to list on **Nasdaq** under the ticker **"ANT,"** after confidentially filing back in June, per reporting from Yahoo Finance and TheStreet. The disclosure reset investor sentiment almost single-handedly: the **Nasdaq Composite** rose **0.82%** to **26,169.38**, the **S&P 500** gained **0.70%** to **7,676.80**, and the **Dow** added **0.28%** to **52,361.52**, with **Nvidia** up **2.39%** to **$216**.

The numbers inside the filing explain the attention. Anthropic reported **$16.3 billion** in revenue for the six months ended June—up from **$4.6 billion** for all of 2025—while spending **$9 billion** on AI infrastructure in the first half alone and posting an **$80.7 billion** net loss driven mostly by accounting charges. The filing also disclosed that about **one-fifth** of revenue came from just two customers, and that the company has agreed to spend up to **$120.5 billion** with **SpaceX's** AI unit through 2029, roughly **$3.1 billion a month** once capacity is delivered starting in December. Shareholders reportedly expect a valuation approaching **$2 trillion**, which would make it one of the largest IPOs in history, rivaling SpaceX's record June listing.

### SpaceX Borrows $40 Billion to Buy AI Chips

The second-largest money move of the day showed the same appetite from the other side of the Anthropic deal. **SpaceX** secured **$40 billion** in debt financing to purchase AI chips, with first shipments expected in the **first half of 2027**, per TheStreet—capital that underwrites the AI compute unit Anthropic just committed to spending up to $120.5 billion with. The scale of the raise underscores how the company is building a data-center business alongside its launch and Starlink operations, and it lands the same week that a separate report had SpaceX in talks to rent tens of thousands of cell sites to enter wireless.

The market took it in stride, with SpaceX shares roughly flat near **$146.79**. But the financing is a reminder that the AI buildout is increasingly being paid for with borrowed money rather than operating cash flow—a dynamic that has made investors nervous about the "neocloud" landlords and is now reaching even the deepest-pocketed private names. For Anthropic, the arrangement effectively locks in a dedicated supplier of compute; for SpaceX, it turns that demand into a financeable, long-dated revenue stream.

### Update: Meta Launches a $95 Billion AI Capital Raise

Having earlier signaled it would build a currency to fund its AI bet, **Meta** moved to actually raise the money. The company plans to raise up to **$95 billion** for AI infrastructure—**$35 billion** through underwritten offerings of common and preferred stock and as much as **$60 billion** through an at-the-market program, per TheStreet and Bloomberg. Notably, chipmakers **Nvidia**, **AMD**, and **Broadcom** may buy up to **$5 billion** of the offering, an unusual arrangement in which suppliers help finance a customer's purchases from them.

The genuinely new detail is the structure and the buyers, not the ambition, which was already known. Meta shares slipped about **1.7%** in early trading on the dilution concern, even as the broader AI complex rallied—a small sign that investors will still penalize the spending they are otherwise cheering elsewhere.

## GNSS News

### TrustPoint and Calian Validate a Mass-Market-Ready C-Band GNSS Antenna

The freshest positioning story pushed GPS-independent navigation toward the consumer shelf. **TrustPoint** said antenna specialist **Calian** has demonstrated what it calls the industry's first commercially manufacturable **C-band GNSS antenna**, acquiring and reliably tracking TrustPoint's live positioning signal from orbit across multiple tests, per SatNews and GPS World (**October 14**). Where today's GPS rides the crowded **L-band**, C-band sits in a less congested part of the spectrum that offers inherent jamming resistance, supports smaller antennas, reduces ionospheric error, and improves availability in urban canyons—advantages TrustPoint frames as a complement to traditional GNSS rather than a replacement.

The milestone matters because an affordable antenna is the gating item for mass adoption of any new signal. TrustPoint is building an end-to-end PNT system of **four satellites and four ground stations** under a U.S. Space Force **Tactical Funding Increase** contract, targeting live trilateration and initial operational services within **12 months**, and is developing a multi-band antenna with Calian that would receive L1/L5 and C-band together. "I anticipate a future where C-band GNSS is integrated into every device," said TrustPoint founder and CEO **Patrick Shannon**—a bet that resilience will eventually be built into phones and cars, not just defense hardware.

### Ground Control's RockBLOCK APNT Flags Spoofing in Under a Second

The complementary story came in a form factor small enough to bolt onto a drone. **Ground Control** launched the **RockBLOCK APNT**, a compact positioning, navigation, and timing board that derives an independent position and timing fix from **Iridium** low-Earth-orbit signals so a host system can cross-check its primary GPS, per GPS World and Unmanned Systems Technology (**October 17**). In controlled tests at **Jammertest 2026**, standard GNSS positions drifted away from a vehicle's known route while the board's fix held steady—and the divergence became detectable **within one second**.

The engineering tradeoffs are built for autonomy. The board is **receive-only** with no internal GNSS receiver, leaning on Iridium signals the company says are about **1,000 times stronger** than GNSS and cryptographically authenticated, packed into a **59 × 36.5 × 7.7 mm**, **10-gram** package drawing barely a watt. It delivers median accuracy of **25 meters at 69°N**, a 1PPS output with timing stability under **50 nanoseconds**, and targets UAVs, uncrewed surface vessels, and robotics. "GNSS is fundamental to autonomous systems, but dependence on a single source of position creates a vulnerability," said CEO **Alastair MacLeod**—the month's running lesson, now small enough to embed anywhere.
