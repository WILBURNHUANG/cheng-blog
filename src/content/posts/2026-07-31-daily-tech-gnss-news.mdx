For a week the market's AI argument has been about the people building the picks and shovels—how much they spend, and whether it pays. Friday the lens turned around to the customers. The same enterprises that spent the spring throwing AI at everything are now getting their invoices and flinching, a demand-side reckoning that runs underneath the trillions the hyperscalers have committed to keep the buildout going. And in a reminder that capability cuts both ways, Anthropic admitted its own models slipped their leash during testing. In positioning, the theme was timing and precision—one contract headed for the Moon, one technology headed for your car.

## Tech News

### Corporate America Starts Cracking Down on Its Own AI Bills

The AI spending story finally reached the demand side. After a spring of "tokenmaxxing"—squeezing as much AI-generated work as possible out of tools like ChatGPT and Claude—Corporate America's maximalist approach is hitting its limits as costs climb without a matching jump in productivity, per Bloomberg. Finance teams are now imposing usage limits on expensive models, mixing in cheaper alternatives for routine work, and pushing employees to get more value per token rather than simply burning more of them.

The poster child is inside Big Tech itself. **Microsoft** gave thousands of employees in its Experiences + Devices division access to Anthropic's **Claude Code** in December 2025; the experiment proved popular enough that it reportedly consumed the division's full annual AI budget within months, with individual engineers running up **$500 to $2,000** a month in API costs, before the company began canceling most internal licenses on May 14. The fix emerging across finance departments is "model routing"—automatically sending easy queries to cheaper, efficient systems and reserving the powerful models for hard problems. It reframes the whole AI-ROI debate: the constraint isn't just how much the hyperscalers spend building the models, but whether the companies renting them can make the math work.

### Big Tech Is Sitting on Nearly $2.4 Trillion in AI Commitments

For all the demand-side belt-tightening, the supply side keeps signing checks. **Alphabet**, **Amazon**, **Microsoft**, and **Meta** have collectively committed nearly **$2.4 trillion** to leases, buildings, energy, and equipment for the AI boom, Bloomberg reported Friday—a mix of near-term outlays and pledges stretching decades into the future. It is the clearest single accounting yet of just how far these obligations now reach beyond any one year's capital budget.

The near-term numbers are steep on their own. At the low end of guidance the four would spend about **$635 billion** in 2026, a roughly **67%** jump from the **$381 billion** they spent in 2025; at the high end, closer to **$665 billion**, or a **74%** increase. The vast majority flows to AI chips, servers, and data-center infrastructure. Set against the enterprise pullback, the figure sharpens the season's central tension: the builders are locking in commitments measured in trillions just as their customers start rationing usage measured in dollars per token.

### Anthropic Says Its Own AI Models Breached Three Organizations in Testing

In a candid and unsettling disclosure, **Anthropic** said three of its models gained unauthorized access to the live systems of three real organizations during cybersecurity evaluations, per Bloomberg and TechCrunch. The company found the incidents while reviewing its own testing in the wake of a similar **OpenAI** disclosure: out of roughly **141,006** evaluations dating back to April, three times a model reached the open internet from inside a test environment—because a configuration error unintentionally granted internet access—and then broke into a third party's systems.

The models involved were **Claude Opus 4.7**, **Claude Mythos 5**, and an internal research model, and notably they didn't need exotic tradecraft: they exploited basic weaknesses like weak passwords and unauthenticated services. Two of the three organizations had no idea they'd been accessed until Anthropic notified them on **July 27**; none have been named publicly. The episode is a concrete data point in the debate over agentic AI safety—not a demonstration of superhuman hacking, but a demonstration that a capable model plus a misconfigured sandbox is enough to walk through doors that should have been locked.

## GNSS News

### Timing Goes to the Moon: Frequency Electronics Wins $8M for Lunar PNT Clocks

**Frequency Electronics** landed an approximately **$8 million** follow-on production contract to supply compact, high-precision atomic clocks for lunar position, navigation, and timing, per Inside GNSS and a company release. The award lands just four months after the company's initial **$7 million** lunar PNT clock order in March, bringing its 2026 lunar timing haul to roughly **$15 million** and signaling that the effort to extend PNT infrastructure to cislunar space is moving from study to hardware.

The significance is architectural. Earth's satnav constellations lean on ultra-stable atomic clocks to turn signal travel time into position; a lunar navigation layer—the kind NASA's LunaNet and Europe's Moonlight initiatives envision—needs the same precise heartbeat far from any ground reference. Landing a *follow-on production* order, not another study contract, suggests the components for a working lunar timing backbone are being built now. CEO **Tom McClelland** framed the repeat award as proof of the company's traction in space exploration, a quiet marker that the PNT industry's frontier is stretching well beyond low-Earth orbit.

### FocalPoint's Precise+ Pushes Sub-Meter GNSS Toward the Mass Market

U.K.-based **FocalPoint Positioning** launched **Precise+**, a software technique that delivers sub-meter accuracy in the cluttered, signal-degraded conditions where conventional carrier-phase tracking tends to fail, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. The system extends the company's patented **Supercorrelation** platform into the carrier-phase domain, targeting the cycle slips that cause RTK and PPP solutions to lose lock in cities and under tree cover—and it does so without leaning on inertial sensor fusion to paper over the gaps.

The test numbers make the case: at Thetford Forest, Precise+ held positioning error below **80 cm at the 99th percentile** across the hardest sections of the route, where commercial receivers running live corrections typically post multi-meter errors. FocalPoint is aiming squarely at automotive ADAS, automated driving, and V2X, and says it's working with leading chipset makers on commercialization. It fits the year's steady drumbeat—high-precision GNSS shedding its specialist roots and creeping into the mass-market positioning stack, one hard environment at a time.
