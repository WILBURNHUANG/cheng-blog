Monday's news kept circling the same question: as AI's appetite for chips, power, and compute keeps compounding, who owns each layer of the machine—and who just gets paid to keep it running? A Japanese lab said it will go public to build its own silicon rather than rent Nvidia's. Taiwan's biggest assembler showed that the servers are now its main business, not the phones. And investors kept throwing money at the software that sits on top. In positioning, the throughline was trust decaying at the edges: a NATO coastline where the satellite signal is jammed most days now, and a receiver maker deciding the only reliable clock is the one you keep in the building.

## Tech News

### Japan's Preferred Networks Will Seek an IPO to Mass-Produce Its Own AI Chips

**Preferred Networks**, one of Japan's few privately held companies worth more than a billion dollars, said it plans to go public to fund mass production of custom AI chips designed to run models more efficiently than general-purpose GPUs, per Bloomberg. CEO **Daisuke Okanohara** said in a Monday interview that the listing is likely **three to five years** out—timed to when chip production actually scales rather than to any near-term cash need—and that the Tokyo company wants foreign investors for the first time in its history, after a decade funded almost entirely by Japanese backers like Toyota, Fanuc, and NTT.

The strategy is a bet against the GPU default. Preferred plans to ship samples of its newest chips to customers in the **first half of next year**, working with **TSMC** on 12nm and 7nm processes and pushing toward **Samsung's** 2nm line for the generation after. It's the same instinct driving hyperscalers to design their own accelerators—own the silicon, escape the Nvidia tax—but coming from a national-champion research lab that now sees custom hardware, not just software, as the thing worth taking public.

### Foxconn's AI-Server Business Now Outsells Everything Else It Makes

**Hon Hai (Foxconn)** reported August sales of **NT$921.77 billion (about $29.1 billion)**, up nearly **52%** year over year and the second-highest monthly figure in its history, driven by AI-server demand, per Focus Taiwan. The cloud-and-networking division—where those servers live—has become the company's center of gravity, crossing **50% of total revenue** for the first time earlier in 2026 and now rivaling the consumer-electronics operations that made Foxconn Apple's largest contract manufacturer.

The number reframes what Foxconn is. Cumulative revenue for the first eight months hit **NT$6.51 trillion**, up nearly **40%** on the year, and the surge is concentrated in racks of GPUs bound for data centers rather than phones bound for stores. For a company long treated as a proxy for iPhone demand, the AI buildout has quietly rewritten the income statement—and made its monthly sales one of the cleaner real-world reads on whether the server boom is still accelerating.

### Cognition Draws Nearly $10 Billion of Interest for a Round at a $47 Billion Valuation

AI coding startup **Cognition**, maker of the Devin software agent, is set to raise around **$1 billion** at a valuation of roughly **$47 billion**, and may take more after fielding nearly **$10 billion** of investor interest, per Bloomberg. The raise would sharply mark up a company that was valued far lower earlier in the year, underscoring how concentrated venture money has become at the frontier of autonomous coding.

The demand says as much about the market as the company. A small cluster of model, chip, and autonomy names has absorbed an outsized share of 2026's venture capital, and coding agents—software that writes and ships code with limited human direction—have become the category investors most want exposure to. Whether $47 billion proves prescient or frothy, the size of the oversubscription is its own signal: capital is racing to back the tools that could automate the very engineers building them.

## GNSS News

### GNSS Jamming Intensifies Along Poland's Baltic Coast—Now on Most Days

Poland's **National Institute of Telecommunications** reported a sharp escalation in deliberate GNSS interference around the **Tri-City** area of Gdańsk, Gdynia, and Sopot, with its Gdańsk monitoring station recording disruption on nearly **two-thirds of days** so far this month, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. Interference across all GNSS bands has climbed steadily since the start of 2026, appearing both as multi-hour, full-spectrum outages and as narrower single-band disruptions, with summer days showing significant degradation somewhere between **39% and 70%** of the time.

The disruption is no longer an occasional anomaly—it's the ambient condition. Researchers from Gdynia Maritime University and Germany's DLR have traced the Baltic jamming to Russian electronic-warfare sites near Kaliningrad, and the everyday fallout is visible in failed location systems on city bikes, scooters, and municipal fleets. Poland's labs have moved from documenting the problem to fielding it: deploying sensor networks and detection tools to protect aviation, maritime traffic, and critical infrastructure in a corridor where operators now have to assume GPS may simply not be there.

### u-blox Wires Switzerland's National Atomic Time Into Its GNSS Test Lab

Positioning-chip maker **u-blox**, together with Switzerland's Federal Institute of Metrology **METAS** and the research network **Switch**, integrated the country's UTC-traceable timing infrastructure directly into u-blox's GNSS test laboratory in **Thalwil**, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The setup pipes **UTC(CH)**—Switzerland's official contribution to Coordinated Universal Time—into the lab over a fiber-optic link using **White Rabbit** technology, giving u-blox continuous, in-house access to a **nanosecond-accurate** reference for validating GNSS timing receivers.

The point is quality control for the clock. GNSS receivers increasingly earn their keep as timing sources for mobile networks, power grids, and financial systems, and validating them used to mean repeated trips to an external metrology facility. Bringing the national time standard onto the bench lets u-blox check its timing products against a sovereign reference on demand—a quieter, infrastructural response to the same anxiety driving the resilience work elsewhere: if GNSS timing is going to underpin critical systems, someone has to prove the timing is right.
