Friday split the difference between good news and bad news, and the market chose to hear only the part it liked. The July jobs report came in ugly—employers actually cut positions, and the back-months got revised lower—but investors read a soft labor market as a green light for rate cuts and sent Big Tech back to record highs. In positioning, the through-line was where tomorrow's navigation signal will come from: a mass-market chipmaker probing low-Earth-orbit augmentation, and a defense team resurrecting a ground-based radio-navigation system built to work when the satellites go dark.

## Tech News

### Employers Unexpectedly Cut Jobs in July, and the Rate-Cut Bets Pile In

The U.S. labor market delivered a genuine shock on Friday: nonfarm payrolls **fell by 23,000** in July, the first outright decline in months, against economist forecasts of roughly **83,000** gains, per Bloomberg, CNBC, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Making the miss worse, May and June were revised down by a combined **103,000** jobs, suggesting the market had been weaker than anyone realized. The unemployment rate actually ticked *down* to **4.1%** from 4.2%—but for the unflattering reason that the labor force shrank as people stopped working or looking.

The internals were soft across the board. Losses clustered in local-government education, which shed **50,000** positions, and retail trade, which lost **19,000**, per the BLS. Wage growth cooled too: average hourly earnings rose just **3.2%** over the past year, the slowest pace since May 2021. For markets, a weak jobs print reads as a dovish one—Seeking Alpha and others noted the report meaningfully boosted the odds of a September rate cut—and that, more than the labor-market weakness itself, is what set Friday's tone on Wall Street.

### Big Tech Storms Back as the AI Euphoria Resumes

After spending much of the year in the doghouse over their AI spending, the world's biggest technology companies are suddenly leading again. Bloomberg reported Friday that the trigger was a pair of earnings showing AI-related revenue growth *accelerating* at **Microsoft** and **Amazon**—until recently two of the group's worst performers—which flipped the narrative from "reckless capex" back to "the buildout is paying off." The rebound erased the anxiety that had rattled markets earlier in the week.

The tape backed it up. The **S&P 500** rose 0.62% to a record close of **7,757.64**, the **Nasdaq Composite** jumped **1.3%** to 26,690.62, and the **Dow** added about 152 points, per CNBC. It's a striking about-face from the volatility that has whipsawed AI names all summer—the same trade that looked fragile days ago now back at all-time highs, powered by the argument that the spending is finally showing up as revenue rather than just cost.

### Astera Labs Prints a 104% Quarter as Its AI Switches Ramp Early

**Astera Labs**, a supplier of the connectivity chips that stitch AI accelerators together inside data centers, reported record second-quarter revenue of **$392.4 million**, up **104%** year over year and **27%** sequentially, comfortably ahead of the roughly **$361 million** consensus, per the company's release and Futurum. PCIe 6 products contributed more than half of revenue, and the Aries signal-conditioning line set a quarterly record of its own.

The louder signal was the guidance. Management steered fiscal third-quarter revenue to **$540–$560 million**—implying about **40%** sequential growth at the midpoint—as its **Scorpio X** fabric switches ramp into the company's largest product family a full quarter earlier than expected, with early 800G Taurus shipments layered on top. In a week where the market rewarded AI names that can show real demand behind the spending, Astera's print was exactly the kind of supply-side confirmation the bulls wanted.

## GNSS News

### u-blox Probes Whether LEO Satellites Can Backstop Mass-Market GNSS

Following the launch of the European Space Agency's first **Celeste** low-Earth-orbit demonstration satellites on March 28, **u-blox** has begun a technical evaluation of how LEO-PNT signals could complement—not replace—the established GNSS constellations that consumer devices already lean on, per Inside GNSS, GPS World, and a u-blox release. Working under ESA's **NAVISP Element 2** program, the company is doing early integration on its **X20** platform, studying how LEO signals across various frequency bands interact with and augment systems like Galileo.

The angle here is mass-market resilience, which is what makes it distinct from the premium and defense LEO-PNT efforts. Celeste is an 11-satellite in-orbit demonstrator—developed through parallel contracts led by **GMV** with **OHB**, and by **Thales Alenia Space**, involving more than 50 entities across 14-plus countries—meant to test whether stronger, lower-orbit signals can harden positioning against jamming and spoofing. u-blox's job is the unglamorous downstream question: can any of this be folded into the cheap, power-constrained receivers that go into phones, cars, and trackers? If the answer is yes, LEO augmentation stops being an exotic add-on and starts becoming a standard resilience feature.

### eLoran Comes Back as a Deploy-From-a-Truck GNSS Backup

On the terrestrial side, **NautelNav** joined a new effort to build a **field-deployable eLoran station**—a portable, ground-based navigation transmitter designed to stand in when GPS is jammed or spoofed, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The pitch is that a low-frequency radio-navigation system built on the Loran-C lineage runs on powerful ground transmitters rather than faint signals from orbit, making it far harder to disrupt than satellite GNSS; a collapsible antenna and a rapidly deployable rig mean a unit can be trucked into a contested area and switched on.

The concept sits under a **£6 million** UK Ministry of Defence contract, with **QinetiQ** leading "Team Elaris"—which includes Nautel partner **UrsaNav**, **Roke**, and **GMV**—as part of the two-year **Urgent Compass** program. At the core is a Nautel eLoran transmitter featuring hot-swappable, redundant power amplifiers and a patented pulse-power-recovery technique. It's the counterweight to the LEO story above: where u-blox looks up for more signals, this team looks to the ground for a signal that keeps working when the sky can't be trusted—two sides of the same resilience problem that has defined the year in PNT.
