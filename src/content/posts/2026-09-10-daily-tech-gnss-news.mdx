Thursday's news split neatly between the thing you can hold and the thing you can't stop buying. Apple finally shipped the foldable it has teased for years, betting a $2,000 phone can reset the upgrade cycle. Oracle showed where the real money is going: a backlog measured in hundreds of billions, booked faster than it can pour concrete. Anthropic picked a very public fight over who trained on whom, and Microsoft admitted its bottleneck is now gigawatts, not GPUs. In positioning, the throughline was self-reliance at the sensor level—teaching a vehicle to keep its bearings when the satellite signal thins out, whether on a London high street or a contested battlefield.

## Tech News

### Apple Unveils the iPhone Duo, Its First Foldable, Starting at $1,999

**Apple** unveiled the **iPhone Duo**, its first foldable phone, at a launch event Wednesday, opening a new product line at **$1,999** and up, per Bloomberg. The passport-style device carries a **5.4-inch** cover display and unfolds to a **7.6-inch** interior screen—Apple's largest-ever iPhone display, with about **50% more** screen area than the iPhone 18 Pro Max—powered by the new **A20 Pro** chip in what Apple calls its thinnest iPhone yet. Pre-orders open **October 16** and the Duo goes on sale **October 23** in more than 70 countries.

The strategic question is whether a folding iPhone can revive a stagnant upgrade cycle at a price that doubles the flagship. Apple entered the category years after Samsung and China's Honor and Huawei, and pitched the Duo as fixing "what people dislike about folding displays"—the crease, the bulk, the fragility. At $2,000 it won't be a volume product, but it reestablishes Apple as a hardware pace-setter at a moment when most of the industry's attention, and capital, has drifted to AI infrastructure. The tell will be whether the crowd that skipped the last three iPhones decides this is the one worth doubling down on.

### Oracle's Cloud Backlog Swells to $664 Billion as AI Demand Outruns Supply

**Oracle** reported fiscal first-quarter results that topped Wall Street on triple-digit cloud-infrastructure growth, with its remaining performance obligations—the contracted backlog—jumping to **$664 billion**, an **$85 billion** sequential increase, per Traders Agency and Yahoo Finance. Total cloud revenue rose **62%** to **$11.61 billion**, cloud-infrastructure revenue climbed **121%** to **$7.4 billion**, and the company said it booked more than **$30 billion** of new AI cloud contracts in the quarter alone.

The number that matters is the gap between demand and delivery. Oracle explicitly said customer appetite for AI training and inference capacity "continues to grow faster than supply," and the quarter showed a sharp jump in capital spending and a widening cash burn as it races to stand up data centers. That is the whole AI-infrastructure trade in one earnings release: revenue visibility years out, matched by an equally enormous bill to build the capacity to fulfill it. Oracle has repositioned itself from legacy database vendor to one of the AI buildout's essential landlords—if it can finance the concrete fast enough.

### Anthropic Accuses China's Moonshot of Secretly Routing 23 Million Requests Through Claude

**Anthropic** published a threat report Thursday accusing Chinese AI champion **Moonshot AI** of covertly routing thousands of user requests to Anthropic's **Claude** models—chiefly the **Opus** tier—and passing the responses off as its own **Kimi** output, per Bloomberg. In one instance Anthropic said it spotted nearly **300,000** customer requests diverted to its models, and attributed more than **23 million** exchanges to Moonshot between May and July. Because Anthropic bars access from inside China, it says Moonshot used **5,380** fraudulent accounts, mostly appearing to originate in Singapore and Japan.

Anthropic framed the activity as **distillation**—using a stronger model's outputs to train a weaker one—and the report (which also named DeepSeek, per the South China Morning Post) marks a sharp escalation in the rivalry between U.S. labs and their fast-rising, cheaper Chinese counterparts. The accusation is hard to fully verify from the outside, and Moonshot has built genuine standing with its low-cost Kimi models. But the dispute crystallizes the industry's next fault line: as frontier labs spend billions training models, the question of who is allowed to learn from whose outputs is becoming a competitive and geopolitical flashpoint, not just a licensing footnote.

## GNSS News

### Jaguar Land Rover Demonstrates Factor-Graph Navigation for GNSS-Denied Driving

The sharpest "keep driving when the sky goes dark" story came from an automaker's research bench. **Jaguar Land Rover** demonstrated a proof-of-concept navigation platform, the **Factor Graph Optimisation Navigation Unit (FGO-Nav)**, built under the European Space Agency's **NAVISP** program to keep vehicles localized where GNSS is degraded, obstructed, or missing—urban canyons, tunnels, and stretches of intermittent satellite visibility, per Inside GNSS. Rather than a conventional Kalman filter, FGO folds GNSS, IMU, and motion-model measurements into a graph of position and velocity states, then uses a nonlinear least-squares optimizer to minimize the residuals—an approach JLR showed outperforming filter-based methods in exactly the hard environments where cars lose their fix.

The design is deliberately modular: the architecture already supports GNSS and inertial sensors, with provision to fold in LiDAR, cameras, and radar as additional factors. To build and validate it, researchers collected more than **24 hours** of synchronized GNSS/INS data across suburban, light-urban, and deep-urban routes, including tunnels, non-line-of-sight zones, high-rise corridors, and tree-shadowed sections. It's a notable signal that factor-graph optimization—long a research-lab favorite—is moving toward the automotive mainstream, and that a luxury carmaker sees robust GNSS-denied positioning as a feature worth engineering rather than a problem to wave away. The timing is apt, with the **ION GNSS+ 2026** conference opening in Orlando on **September 14**.

### U.S. Army Buys More Dismounted Assured-PNT Kit for Ground Troops

On the defense side, the money kept flowing toward soldier-carried resilience. The U.S. Army's **Project Manager Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PM PNT)** awarded a delivery order worth more than **$3 million** for **Dismounted Assured PNT System (DAPS)** Mounted Engineering Change Proposal (DME) **B-kits**, per GPS World. DAPS is the Army's program for giving dismounted troops trustworthy position and timing in environments where GPS is jammed or spoofed, packaging multiple sensors and anti-jam protections into gear a soldier can carry.

The order is modest in dollars but telling in direction: incremental, sustained procurement rather than a one-off prototype, reflecting how thoroughly assured PNT has become baseline kit for ground forces operating under electronic-warfare threat. It's the same instinct visible across the GNSS world right now—from receiver labs to warfighter pouches—that the raw satellite signal can no longer be assumed present, and that resilience has to be built into the endpoint. For the vendors serving this market, the steady drip of engineering-change and B-kit orders is where a lot of the real, recurring revenue actually lives.
