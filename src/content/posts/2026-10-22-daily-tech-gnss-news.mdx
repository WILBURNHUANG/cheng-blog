Wednesday's news kept circling one idea: in artificial intelligence, the binding constraint has moved from demand to supply. The day's biggest stories were not about whether anyone wants more compute—China's largest internet companies reportedly tried to buy billions of dollars of it—but about whether the chips, the factories, and the test lines can be built fast enough to deliver. Nvidia answered by locking in suppliers years ahead of need, a skeptic answered by calling the whole thing a bubble, and the quarter's earnings answered by showing the AI cycle still lifting old-line names. In positioning, the month's steady lesson held with a new venue: navigation that survives a jammed or spoofed signal moved from the battlefield into the passenger cabin.

## Tech News

### China's Big Three Reportedly Order More Than $35 Billion of Nvidia Inference Cards

The day's defining story was a demand signal so large it exposed the limits of supply. **ByteDance**, **Alibaba**, and **Tencent** have ordered more than **3 million** **Nvidia** **RTX PRO 5500** workstation cards—worth over **$35 billion** at roughly **$11,400** apiece—primarily for AI inference, Reuters reported on **October 20**, citing people familiar with the matter. Total orders from Chinese customers have reportedly passed **6 million** units, with Shenzhen-listed Tansun Technology disclosing a deal for 20,000 of the cards earlier in the month.

The catch is that Nvidia has told customers and partners it "cannot guarantee that it can fill the orders," with one source saying buyers may receive only **5% to 10%** of what they requested. Nvidia says it can produce no more than 3 million of the cards worldwide in 2027, constrained by **TSMC** capacity it expects to commit largely to its next-generation Rubin chips. The RTX PRO 5500 is weaker than the China-barred B300 and the case-by-case-licensed H200, and a Nvidia spokesperson used the orders to argue that "outdated US export controls" have handed Chinese rivals "unprecedented growth"—a reminder that the company's current-quarter guidance of about **$108 billion** explicitly excludes Chinese data-center compute.

### Nvidia Prepays Suppliers and Lines Up Six Partners to Expand Capacity Through 2028

If demand is not the problem, Nvidia spent the day attacking the thing that is. At its **GTC Berlin** conference on **October 21**, the company unveiled capacity-expansion agreements with six supply-chain partners—**Ibiden**, **Samsung Electro-Mechanics**, **Unimicron**, **Amkor**, **SPIL**, and **King Yuan Electronics**—covering the substrates, packaging, testing, and optics that surround its processors, per coverage from Forbes. Nvidia advanced **$1.1 billion** to King Yuan to reserve testing capacity at new U.S. sites and signed a multibillion-dollar advance to Ibiden, paying for capacity roughly two years before it is needed.

The targets are aggressive: substrate capacity up **2.5x** over three years, Amkor's South Korean packaging up more than **300%** by end-2028, testing capacity doubling from 2026 to 2027, and SPIL's optical-engine output up **15x** by mid-2027. In its latest filing, Nvidia disclosed that supply commitments—mostly memory and manufacturing—jumped from **$119 billion** to **$279 billion** in a single quarter. "Capacity is the constraint," CEO **Jensen Huang** said, calling demand "extraordinary"—a framing that turns the week's bubble worries into a bet on execution rather than appetite.

### IBM's Q3 Comes In Strong on AI and Mainframe Demand

The clearest counterweight to the bubble talk came from an old name riding the new cycle. **IBM** reported third-quarter revenue of **$17.1 billion**, up **5%** (6% in constant currency), with **Software** up **8%** to **$7.8 billion** and **Infrastructure** up **4%** to **$3.7 billion** as its latest **IBM Z** mainframe cycle and AI book of business carried the quarter, per the company's October 21 release. **Consulting** was flat at **$5.3 billion**.

Profitability outran the top line: operating (non-GAAP) EPS rose **11%** to **$2.95**, GAAP EPS from continuing operations jumped **26%** to **$2.31**, and free cash flow reached **$3.0 billion** for the quarter and **$7.8 billion** year to date. IBM nudged up its full-year outlook, now expecting free cash flow to rise by more than **$1 billion** year over year while holding constant-currency revenue growth at **4% to 5%**. For a company often read as a proxy for enterprise IT spending, a software-and-mainframe beat suggests the AI buildout is pulling durable demand through to incumbents, not just the chipmakers.

## GNSS News

### Safran's SkyNaute Inertial System Is Selected for the Airbus A220

The freshest positioning news carried resilient navigation out of the military world and onto a commercial airliner. **Safran Electronics & Defense** said its **SkyNaute** inertial navigation system has been selected for the **Airbus A220**—its first time flying on an airliner—pairing inertial sensing with hybridized GNSS data to hold a precise trajectory even when satellite signals are "unavailable, jammed, or spoofed," per GPS World (**October 20**). The system is built around Safran's patented **hemispherical resonator gyroscope (HRG)**, which has no mechanical parts subject to wear and is lighter, smaller, and lower-power than earlier designs.

The move matters because civil aviation is increasingly treating GNSS interference as an operational reality rather than an edge case. SkyNaute is already fielded on military aircraft, helicopters, and drones across several European forces; bringing it to a mainstream narrowbody signals that airlines now want inertial resilience built in, not bolted on. Safran is backing the demand with capacity: a **€120 million** investment announced in June will triple HRG production at its Montluçon site from **10,000 to 30,000** units a year by **2032**.

### Skyband Systems Puts Its Iridium-Based M100 Anti-Spoofing Avionics Into Production

The complementary story came from a different layer of the same problem. **Skyband Systems** announced that its **M100** avionics unit—which fuses **Iridium** low-Earth-orbit PNT with onboard inertial sensing to alert flight crews to GPS jamming and spoofing—has entered production, per GPS World (**October 19**). The box taps an aircraft's existing Iridium antenna and feeds the flight deck through an electronic flight bag app and a cockpit annunciator, providing position and timing independent of local GPS availability.

The significance is how quickly it is moving toward certified service. Skyband says the M100's end-to-end performance was validated across **17 test flights**, with qualification testing slated for October and a first FAA Supplemental Type Certificate—on an **Embraer Praetor 600**—targeted for **November 2026**. The urgency is in the numbers the company cites: GPS spoofing incidents rose **193%** and jamming events **67%** between 2023 and 2025, according to IATA. Like Safran's HRG, it reflects the month's running GNSS theme crossing into aviation—survivability comes from an independent second source of truth that can catch the primary signal lying.
