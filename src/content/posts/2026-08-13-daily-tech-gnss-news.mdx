Thursday was about the AI race widening past the silicon layer—into acquisitions, talent, and the phones people carry. Anthropic reached for its biggest deal yet, OpenAI hired a sales boss to prime its IPO, and Google shipped the first 2nm smartphone chip. The counter-melody was the cost of it all: an AI-hardware name got punished for a soft quarter, and the tech sector's debt binge started bleeding into credit spreads well beyond AI. In positioning, the through-line was a jammable sky and the scramble to answer it—one side building the weapon that denies GPS, the other testing a low-orbit layer meant to make navigation harder to knock out.

## Tech News

### Anthropic Weighs Its Largest-Ever Deal: ~$6 Billion for World-Model Startup Decart

**Anthropic** is in talks to acquire AI startup **Decart** for about **$6 billion**, which would be by far the largest acquisition ever made by the company behind Claude, per Bloomberg. Decart, founded in 2023 by three Israeli engineers, builds "world models" that simulate physical environments and real-time generative video, alongside software that makes AI chips run more efficiently—and it's that second piece that reportedly matters most to Anthropic, whose infrastructure could use the efficiency gains to absorb more demand. Decart's team would join Anthropic's inference and performance group if a deal closes.

The price tag tells the strategic story. A recent round valued Decart at almost **$4 billion** (up from **$3.1 billion** a year earlier), so a **$6 billion** deal is roughly a **50%** premium, per Fortune and the Wall Street Journal's reporting. Coming as Anthropic prepares for a stock-market listing, the move reads as a bet that owning chip-optimization technology—rather than renting more compute—is the cheaper path to scaling Claude. The caveat: talks are early and could still fall apart, per Bloomberg.

### Google Launches the Pixel 11 Line on Tensor G6—the First 2nm Phone Chip

**Google** unveiled the **Pixel 11, Pixel 11 Pro, Pixel 11 Pro XL** and a **Pro Fold** on August 12, all powered by the new **Tensor G6**—the first smartphone processor built on **TSMC's 2nm** node, per TechPowerUp and GSMArena. Google says the chip improves web-browsing performance by about **25%** and app-launch speed by **15%** over the Tensor G5, with a much larger TPU for on-device AI, an upgraded image processor, and a new Titan M3 security chip. The Pro models add a redesigned camera with **120x** zoom and a color LED strip called **HiLight** that surfaces calls, messages, and Gemini activity.

The interesting part is the process node. Being first to 2nm gives Google—and TSMC—a visible showcase for the manufacturing generation the whole industry is racing toward, and it leans hard into on-device Gemini as the Pixel's selling point rather than raw benchmarks. Pre-orders are open, with sales starting August 20 and the 256GB base model priced at **€1,399**, per Gizmochina.

### OpenAI Hires Wiz's Dali Rajic as Revenue Chief Ahead of Its Market Debut

**OpenAI** named **Dali Rajic**—president and COO of Alphabet-owned cybersecurity firm **Wiz**—as its new chief revenue officer, its second CRO in under a year, per Bloomberg. The hire is aimed squarely at driving enterprise sales growth as the company preps a highly anticipated Wall Street debut, bringing in an operator with a track record of scaling go-to-market machines to help turn ChatGPT's reach into durable commercial revenue.

The churn at the top of the sales org is worth noting—cycling through two revenue chiefs in a year suggests OpenAI is still figuring out how to sell at the scale its valuation implies. But pulling a senior leader out of one of the hottest names in security signals how seriously it's taking the enterprise motion as the IPO narrative comes into focus.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Cerebras slides on a revenue miss**: AI-chip maker **Cerebras Systems** fell about **14%** after second-quarter revenue of **$180.1 million** came in below the **$193.6 million** consensus and down **7%** year over year, with an adjusted loss of **$2.98** per share, per Yahoo Finance and CNBC—though core revenue (excluding data-center pass-through) rose **103%** to **$209.9 million** and cloud revenue jumped **281%**, and the company raised full-year core guidance to **$880–890 million**. A reminder that "lumpy" AI-hardware demand can still punish a stock even mid-boom.
- **Tech's debt binge spills into safe-name credit**: A flood of bond sales by Big Tech is inadvertently pushing up credit-default-swap costs even for companies with no ties to data centers or AI, per Bloomberg—an early sign the sheer scale of the buildout's financing is rippling into corners of the credit market that never signed up for the AI trade.
- **The AI earnings gauntlet is set**: The next leg of the AI trade runs through late August, when the biggest infrastructure names report—**Nvidia** on August 26, **Marvell** on August 27, and **Broadcom** on September 2, per market previews—setting up the results that will test whether the summer's rally has more room to run.

## GNSS News

### U.S. Forces Put Their Maritime "Hammer of the Gods" GPS-Denial System to Sea

The military spent the summer building the offensive side of the navigation-warfare problem. The **U.S. Army, Navy, and Space Force** deployed a maritime version of an electronic-warfare system called **Hammer of the Gods** to sea for the first time in mid-July during **Project Convergence Capstone 6** (PC-C6), the Army's large-scale joint experimentation event, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The system is a highly deployable, low-electromagnetic-signature emitter that delivers command-and-control and EW effects at the forward edge—and its purpose is to jam **positioning, navigation, and timing** signals, including GPS, that precision-guided weapons and uncrewed systems rely on to navigate.

The maritime debut is the news. Land-based versions have shown up in exercises over the past two years, but taking the capability to open water—as part of a joint experiment run with Australian, New Zealand, British, and Canadian forces at Fort Irwin and at sea—extends GPS-denial into naval and littoral operations. It's a telling counterpoint to the year's steady drumbeat of civil jamming alarm: here the same physics is the objective, a reminder that resilient PNT matters precisely because degrading an adversary's navigation is now a fielded, practiced mission rather than a theoretical threat.

### u-blox Starts Testing Whether LEO Signals Can Harden Mass-Market Navigation

On the defensive, commercial side, the interesting move is toward a new orbital layer. **u-blox** said it is evaluating how **low-Earth-orbit (LEO)** signals can complement GNSS for mass-market positioning, prompted by the March launch of the European Space Agency's first **Celeste** LEO-PNT demonstration satellites, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. Working under ESA's **NAVISP** program, u-blox is characterizing how LEO signals interact with established constellations like Galileo and testing integration on its **X20** GNSS platform.

The rationale is physical. LEO satellites orbit far closer than the ~20,000 km GNSS constellations, so their signals arrive stronger and their geometry shifts fast—qualities that can speed positioning convergence and add robustness in tough signal environments (urban canyons, foliage, light interference). u-blox is careful to frame LEO not as a GNSS replacement but as an additional layer, and the full Celeste demo will eventually field **11** satellites across multiple bands. For a mass-market chip vendor to invest engineering in LEO now is a signal in itself: the industry increasingly sees a low-orbit augmentation layer—not just better receivers—as part of how everyday positioning gets more reliable and harder to spoof.
