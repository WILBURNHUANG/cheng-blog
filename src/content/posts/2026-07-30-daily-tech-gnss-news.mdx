After a brutal stretch that had the market interrogating every dollar of AI spending, Thursday delivered the sharpest reversal of the season. A blowout cloud print from one hyperscaler was all it took to flip sentiment: chips that had been left for dead came screaming back, the Nasdaq snapped a six-day losing streak, and a single stock rewrote the record book for one-day value creation. Then the after-hours session piled on, with Amazon and Apple headlining a night that showed the "AI trade" splintering into winners and question marks. In positioning, the theme was unmistakably military—two announcements that treat GPS itself as a weapon and a target.

## Tech News

### The AI Trade Snaps Back—and Microsoft Rewrites the Record Book

The relief rally that AI bulls had been waiting for finally arrived on Thursday, and it was violent. **Microsoft** shares jumped **16%** on the market reaction to its cloud results, adding roughly **$450 billion** in market value in a single session—the most any stock has ever gained in one day, per Bloomberg. That one move set the tone for a broad rebound: the **Nasdaq Composite** climbed **2.8%** to close at **25,122**, ending a six-day losing streak, while the **S&P 500** rose **1.7%** to **7,437** and the **Dow** gained about **614 points**.

The recovery was most dramatic in the corner that had been punished hardest. Dip buyers stormed back into semiconductors on speculation that the AI trade has more room to run, sending the iShares Semiconductor ETF (**SOXX**) up more than **8%**, with **Micron** soaring roughly **18%** and **AMD** up more than **13%**, per Yahoo Finance and CNBC. It was a near-perfect mirror image of the China-lithography rout earlier in the week—proof that in a tape this jumpy, conviction can swing from despair to euphoria on the strength of one earnings line.

### Amazon's AWS Accelerates to Its Fastest Growth in Four Years

**Amazon** carried the momentum into the after-hours session, beating on both lines and reporting cloud growth that accelerated for a fifth straight quarter. Total net sales rose **20%** to **$200.6 billion**, and earnings per share of **$5.75** blew past the roughly **$1.81** consensus, per CNBC and Yahoo Finance. But the headline was **AWS**: cloud revenue grew **37%** year over year to **$42.23 billion**—its fastest rate in 18 quarters, accelerating from 28% in the prior quarter—with operating income of **$16.6 billion** at a **39.4%** margin.

The AI signal underneath was just as strong: AWS said its artificial-intelligence business and its in-house chips each cleared an annualized run rate of over **$25 billion**, more than doubling from a year ago. The cost of that growth showed up in the cash flow statement—CEO **Andy Jassy** guided full-year capital spending to about **$220 billion**, and trailing-twelve-month free cash flow turned negative for the first time in recent memory at roughly **-$7.6 billion**, per Investing.com. Shares rose in late trading anyway, a sign investors are once again willing to fund the buildout when the demand shows up in the numbers.

### Apple Posts a Record June Quarter, but China and Services Disappoint

**Apple** delivered its strongest June quarter on record, with revenue up **16%** to **$109.4 billion** and diluted EPS up **29%** to **$2.02**, both ahead of the **$1.89** and **$108.8 billion** Wall Street expected, per Yahoo Finance and Quartz. **iPhone** revenue of **$54.25 billion** grew **22%** year over year and topped estimates, the engine of an unusually strong quarter for a device cycle many had written off as mature.

The market focused on the soft spots. Sales in **Greater China** came in at **$18.8 billion**, below the **$19.6 billion** analysts modeled, and **Services** revenue of **$30.74 billion** missed the **$31.22 billion** consensus—two of the metrics investors most want to see hold up. The stock fell roughly **3–4%** in extended trading, a reminder that after briefly touching a $5 trillion valuation this week, Apple is being held to a standard where even a record quarter can disappoint. The report was also the last earnings call for CEO **Tim Cook** before he hands the reins to **John Ternus** on September 1.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Semiconductor revenue set to top $1 trillion for the first time**: Global chip industry revenue will exceed **$1 trillion** in 2026, a historic milestone driven by surging memory and logic demand from AI, per industry trackers cited by Barchart—context for why traders keep buying the dips despite the volatility.
- **Chip names lead the rebound**: The rally was concentrated in the hardest-hit hardware names, with Micron up about **18%** and AMD more than **13%**, as investors reversed the prior week's China-competition and AI-debt fears, per CNBC.

## GNSS News

### 'Hammer of the Gods' Sails: The Joint Force Takes GPS-Denial to Sea

The U.S. **Army**, **Navy**, and **Space Force** put a maritime version of an electronic-warfare system called **Hammer of the Gods** to sea for the first time in mid-July, during **Project Convergence Capstone 6 (PC-C6)**—the Army's large-scale joint experimentation event—off the coast of southern California, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. Hammer of the Gods is a portable, low-electromagnetic-signature emitter built to interfere with positioning, navigation, and timing signals, including GPS. Land-based versions have shown up in exercises over the past two years, but the maritime debut validated all three phases an operational deployment requires: getting the system into the fight, running it in a real maritime environment, and getting it back.

The strategic logic is blunt. GPS and other PNT signals are what make precision-guided weapons—long-range artillery, guided missiles, cruise missiles—precise; take away the navigation data and an adversary's expensive munitions become far less accurate. Pushing a counter-PNT emitter onto a highly deployable sea platform extends that denial capability to the maritime edge, where much of the modern precision-strike fight now plays out. It's the offensive bookend to the resilience theme that has dominated this column for weeks: the same forces racing to keep their own receivers alive are practicing, hard, at killing everyone else's.

### IAI Unveils HYPNOSIS, a Soft-Kill System That Downs Drone Swarms by Corrupting Their GPS

On the industry side, **Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI)** introduced **HYPNOSIS**, a high-power navigation-warfare (NAVWAR) system designed to defend fixed, high-value sites against GNSS-guided aerial threats—chiefly the large drone swarms that have come to define recent conflicts—by attacking their satellite-navigation link rather than shooting them down, per Inside GNSS. The system is built around a network of mobile jamming and spoofing stations arrayed around a protected site, each tied to a central command-and-control unit that can run autonomously, without an operator directing individual stations—a design choice meant to compress response time when a coordinated, multi-directional attack unfolds in seconds.

HYPNOSIS works either by overpowering a drone's GNSS signals with noise (jamming) or by broadcasting counterfeit signals that trick the receiver into computing a false position (spoofing). IAI frames it as the offensive counterpart to its **ADA** GNSS anti-jamming system, a defensive CRPA-based receiver-protection technology it has fielded for over two decades—the same NAVWAR expertise pointed in the opposite direction. Together with the Hammer of the Gods test, it captures where the PNT field is heading in 2026: navigation warfare is no longer a niche concern but a mainstream category, with soft-kill GPS denial emerging as a preferred answer to the cheap-drone problem that missiles can't economically solve.
