Wednesday's news was less about new capabilities than the guardrails and ceilings closing in around them. Broadcom, one of the biggest AI winners, guided below expectations—an early hint that even the custom-silicon boom has a pace. Uber reorganized itself for a future where cars drive themselves. And the frontier labs pulled in opposite directions on the same week: OpenAI flagged a model dangerous enough to lock down, while Anthropic made its agents cheaper to run all day. In positioning, the throughline was intelligence over brute resilience—optimization math that navigates through the gaps GPS leaves, and hardware headed to Army vehicles that assumes the signal will be contested.

## Tech News

### Broadcom's Forecast Misses as the AI Chip Payoff Slows

**Broadcom** gave a fourth-quarter revenue forecast of **$34.8 billion**, just under the roughly **$35.1 billion** analysts expected, with some estimates topping $36 billion—and shares fell after the report, per Bloomberg. The miss is modest in dollars but pointed in meaning: Broadcom has been one of the clearest beneficiaries of the AI buildout, designing custom accelerators for hyperscalers who want an alternative to Nvidia's GPUs, and even a small shortfall reads as a signal the payoff is arriving slower than the most bullish investors priced in.

The context matters more than the number. Where Nvidia captures attention with general-purpose GPUs, Broadcom's niche is bespoke silicon tuned to a single customer's workload, plus the networking chips that stitch clusters together. A soft guide from that corner of the market suggests custom-chip demand, while enormous, is not immune to digestion after a year of frantic ordering—a rare note of gravity in a sector that has mostly known one direction.

### Uber Cuts 3,300 Jobs to Flatten Itself for the Robotaxi Era

**Uber** is laying off about **3,300** employees, roughly **10%** of its global workforce, as CEO **Dara Khosrowshahi** strips out management layers and consolidates teams, per Reuters. Management framed the cuts not as a response to a weakening core business but as a deliberate simplification—too many small teams and coordination layers had slowed decisions—while the company also concentrates staff in major hubs and trims fully remote roles.

The strategic subtext is autonomy. Uber is entering an expensive transition as robotaxis move toward commercial deployment, with **Waymo** and others threatening to rewrite ride-hailing economics. Cutting bureaucracy while redirecting investment toward self-driving partnerships makes this a repositioning rather than a downturn—an incumbent trying to get lean before the technology it depends on reshapes its business.

### OpenAI Rates Its Next Model, Astra, a 'Critical' Cyber Risk

**OpenAI** said its forthcoming **Astra** model has crossed into "Critical" cyber capability under its Preparedness Framework, meaning it can potentially identify previously unknown vulnerabilities and develop exploits against well-defended systems with far less human guidance than existing public models, per The Information. The assessment triggered additional controls on access, monitoring, and deployment, and OpenAI plans to restrict Astra rather than release its full capabilities broadly.

The disclosure is an unusually concrete marker of where the frontier is heading. Coding assistants already help defenders find weaknesses, but a model that autonomously discovers and weaponizes flaws changes the math—lowering the expertise and labor needed to mount sophisticated attacks. It also raises pressure on rival labs to spell out how they will handle offensive security capabilities that are starting to rival skilled human teams.

### Anthropic Cuts Agent Costs Up to 45% With Claude Fable 5.1

**Anthropic** released **Claude Fable 5.1** and **Claude Mythos 5.1**, its latest models for coding, knowledge work, cybersecurity, and science, per The Verge. Fable 5.1 is broadly available and can match or beat its predecessor at lower reasoning settings while cutting the cost of some highly agentic workloads by up to **45%**; the more capable Mythos 5.1 stays restricted to vetted organizations because of its advanced cyber and biology capabilities.

The economics may matter as much as the benchmarks. Enterprises increasingly care about the cost of sustained agentic work, not one-off answers, and Anthropic paired the launch with cheaper cache-read pricing and tighter safeguards meant to stop legitimate security and science requests from being blocked. The visible split between a broadly available model and a locked-down frontier one—the same week OpenAI flagged Astra—looks like an emerging industry template.

## GNSS News

### Jaguar Land Rover Shows Factor-Graph Navigation That Beats Kalman Filters in the Gaps

The most interesting positioning story of the day was about smarter math, not stronger signals. **Jaguar Land Rover** demonstrated a proof-of-concept **Factor Graph Optimisation Navigation Unit (FGO-Nav)** targeting vehicle localization where GNSS is degraded, obstructed, or unavailable, developed under ESA's NAVISP programme, per Inside GNSS. Rather than processing measurements sequentially like a conventional Kalman filter, FGO represents navigation as a graph linking vehicle states, sensor readings, and constraints, then optimizes them jointly over a time window.

The payoff shows up exactly where GPS struggles. JLR collected more than **24 hours** of synchronized GNSS/INS data across suburban, light-urban, and deep-urban settings—tunnels, non-line-of-sight zones, high-rise corridors, tree-shadowed roads—and benchmarked FGO against weighted least squares, extended Kalman filtering, and a commercial RTK receiver, with the graph approach outperforming in the degraded cases like urban canyons and intermittent-visibility stretches. It points to a near-term path for consumer vehicle navigation to stay accurate through the dead zones drivers actually hit, without exotic new hardware.

### U.S. Army Orders First Vehicle-Mounted Assured-PNT Kits

On the defense side, the **U.S. Army's Project Manager Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PM PNT)** awarded an initial delivery order worth more than **$3 million** for **Dismounted Assured PNT System (DAPS) Mounted Engineering Change Proposal (DME)** B-kits, issued through Army Contracting Command–Aberdeen Proving Ground, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. DME combines satellite receivers, an anti-jam antenna, and inertial-based navigation to deliver assured positioning, and is meant to bridge mounted and dismounted platforms for combat-support and combat-service-support crews.

The kits are slated for integration into medium and heavy tactical vehicles in **2027**. It's a small dollar figure but a telling one: the Army is buying gear that assumes the satellite signal will be jammed or spoofed as a baseline condition, extending assured-PNT protection from the dismounted soldier onto the vehicles that carry them.
