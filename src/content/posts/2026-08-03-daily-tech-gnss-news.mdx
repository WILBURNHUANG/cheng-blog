The market opened August the way it closed July—arguing with itself. One camp keeps buying the AI story on the strength of real revenue: Amazon crossed a valuation line only two other companies have reached, and the software layer put up numbers that made even skeptics blink. The other camp keeps eyeing the tab, and this week it got two fresh reminders of how big it's getting. In positioning, the theme was patience rewarded and vigilance demanded—a long-delayed military GPS program finally nearing the finish line, and a civil-aviation regulator tightening its playbook as interference spreads.

## Tech News

### Update: Amazon Joins the $3 Trillion Club

**Amazon** became the third company ever to reach a **$3 trillion** market capitalization on Monday, extending the rally that followed last week's blowout cloud results, per Bloomberg. The milestone—territory previously reached only by the market's largest tech names—caps a remarkable stretch for a stock that spent much of the spring being punished for its capital spending. The new detail here isn't the AWS growth rate that got it there, which the market already digested; it's that investors are now willing to underwrite the buildout at a valuation that puts Amazon in the market's most exclusive tier. The broader tape was less unanimous: U.S. stocks rose into the first trading day of the month, but chip names slipped, with **Micron** and **Broadcom** retreating and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index off about **1.9%**—a reminder that the enthusiasm remains selective.

### Palantir Posts 93% Growth and Calls Demand "Otherworldly"

**Palantir** delivered one of the season's loudest software prints, reporting second-quarter revenue of **$1.935 billion**, up **93%** year over year, and raising its full-year outlook again, per Bloomberg and a company release. The engine was the U.S. business, which grew **115%** and now makes up more than **81%** of revenue, with U.S. commercial revenue accelerating to a striking **149%** year-over-year clip. Management lifted full-year 2026 revenue guidance to roughly **$8.15 billion**—about **82%** growth—and raised its U.S. commercial target to more than **$3.42 billion**, or at least 134% growth, comfortably ahead of Wall Street. CEO **Alex Karp** described commercial demand for the company's data-analytics tools as "otherworldly," the kind of superlative that either ages beautifully or becomes a punchline; for now, the numbers back him up. It's the clearest sign yet that the AI-monetization debate has a software answer, not just an infrastructure one.

### Alibaba Drops Qwen3.8-Max, Claims It Rivals Anthropic

**Alibaba** unveiled **Qwen3.8-Max**, its largest AI model to date, and made the increasingly familiar claim that a Chinese lab has drawn level with the American frontier, per Bloomberg, CNBC, and MarkTechPost. The model is a mixture-of-experts design with **2.4 trillion** total parameters but only about **95 billion** active on any single query—an architecture meant to hold down latency and inference cost—and it supports a context window of up to **1 million tokens** across text, image, and video. Alibaba's own benchmarks show it delivering scores comparable to, and on several coding, multimodal, and engineering tests better than, **Anthropic's** models, while trailing on some general-purpose reasoning. Investors liked the pitch: Alibaba shares rallied on the announcement. Open weights are reportedly due next week—the same open-release strategy that has let Chinese models spread quickly and keep pricing pressure on their U.S. rivals.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Citadel Securities sees a $500 billion chip-debt binge**: The market-making giant forecasts another **$500 billion**-plus of public- and private-market debt by **2028** to finance the chips going into AI data-center campuses, per Bloomberg—a concrete estimate of how much borrowed money now underpins the buildout, and a fresh data point for investors already nervous about AI leverage.
- **Caterpillar limps into earnings after an AI selloff**: **Caterpillar** heads into its report having shed nearly a quarter of its value last month, as a broad retreat from AI-infrastructure stocks dragged down industrial names that sell power and equipment to data centers, per Bloomberg—evidence the AI trade's swings now reach well beyond chips and software.

## GNSS News

### Light at the End of the M-Code Tunnel: Jam-Resistant GPS Receivers Target FY2027

After two decades of delays, the U.S. military can finally see the finish line for its jam- and spoof-resistant GPS receivers. Air Force officials now expect operational testing of the chip cards that let maritime and aviation platforms use the protected **M-code** signal to wrap up in **fiscal 2027**, per Breaking Defense and SatNews. Development is done and the program has moved into operational checkout, according to Col. **Jameson Locklear**, senior materiel leader for the **M-code Aviation Receivers Joint Program Office**. The timing lands two decades after the first M-code-capable satellite launched—an unusually stark illustration of how far receiver development can lag the space segment.

The organizational piece fell into place first. The Space Force and Air Force agreed last year to move the **Military GPS User Equipment (MGUE)** program from Space Systems Command to the Air Force Life Cycle Management Center, and that transition was finalized on **July 17**. The hardest remaining hurdle is at sea: operational testing on the Navy's **DDG-51 Arleigh Burke**-class destroyers has slipped to FY2027 from a prior 2025 target, and AFLCMC is working with Navy counterparts to free up a destroyer around its deployment schedule so testing can finish on time. It's a quieter counterpoint to the navigation-warfare headlines of recent weeks: before you can deny an adversary's PNT, you have to make sure your own force can actually use the hardened signal you've been launching since the 2000s.

### EASA Sharpens Its GNSS Interference Guidance as Spoofing Grows "More Severe"

On the civil side, the **European Union Aviation Safety Agency** issued Revision 4 of its Safety Information Bulletin on GNSS outages and interference on **July 3**, tightening guidance for airlines and manufacturers as jamming and spoofing incidents climb, per EASA and Runway Girl Network. The update draws on the first deliverables of a joint **EASA–EUROCONTROL** task force and warns that spoofing events are growing in both "severity" and sophistication—not just frequency. Regulators say they've tracked a "notable increase" in interference since 2022, concentrated around conflict zones and other sensitive regions.

The bulletin is less a technical breakthrough than a governance signal, and that's the point. Where much of this column's recent coverage has framed GNSS interference as a battlefield tool, EASA's revision underscores how thoroughly the problem has spilled into everyday commercial aviation, where a spoofed position or a lost fix is a passenger-safety issue rather than a tactical one. Stakeholders are urged to adopt the recommended mitigations "as soon as possible"—the unglamorous regulatory work of turning a spreading threat into standardized cockpit and maintenance procedure.

## Key Takeaways

- **The AI trade's optimists had the mic**: Amazon joined Apple and the market's other giants in the $3 trillion club, Palantir grew revenue 93% and lifted full-year guidance to about $8.15 billion on "otherworldly" demand, and Alibaba's 2.4-trillion-parameter Qwen3.8-Max drew a share rally on claims it rivals Anthropic.
- **But the financing bill kept growing**: Citadel Securities projected another $500 billion of chip debt by 2028 to fund AI data centers, and Caterpillar limped into earnings after losing nearly a quarter of its value—while chip names like Micron and Broadcom slipped even as the broader market rose.
- **PNT's story was resilience, military and civil**: The Air Force now targets FY2027 to finish testing jam-resistant M-code GPS receivers for ships and planes—two decades after the first M-code satellite—while EASA sharpened its interference guidance, warning that aviation GNSS spoofing is growing more severe and sophisticated, not just more common.
