Wednesday turned on a single data point and a single show of force. The July inflation report landed right about where economists hoped, taking a September rate hike off the table in most investors' minds and clearing the way for AI names to run again. The bigger story sat one layer down: the market spent the day sorting out who pays for the AI buildout and who collects, with a networking giant raising its AI forecast and a chipmaker assembling a half-trillion-dollar war chest to reassure nervous lenders. In positioning, the through-line was precision timing and shrinking hardware—clocks headed for the Moon, and a resilient receiver small enough to ride on a drone.

## Tech News

### A Benign CPI Clears the Way, and the AI Trade Runs Again

The inflation number that markets had been holding their breath for came in cool. The **consumer price index** rose just **0.1%** in July, putting the annual rate at **3.4%** and matching consensus, while **core CPI**—stripping out food and energy—rose **0.2%** on the month for a **2.5%** annual pace, per CNBC and Yahoo Finance. Coming after a soft July jobs report, the print reinforced bets that the **Federal Reserve** will hold rates steady in September rather than resume hikes, and stocks read it as permission to lean back into risk.

The move was concentrated where it usually is. The **Nasdaq Composite** added about **0.54%** to close near **26,588**, and the **S&P 500** rose **0.26%** to **7,748.50**, per CNBC, as upbeat AI-infrastructure earnings did the heavy lifting. Individual names told the story better than the indexes: **Nebius Group** surged **16.5%** after booking larger AI contracts and raising prices, and **Lumentum** jumped **14.3%** after fiscal-fourth-quarter revenue more than doubled to **$1.01 billion**, up **109%**, with adjusted EPS of **$3.23** beating the **$2.95** consensus, per the earnings coverage. The soft inflation data was the catalyst, but the fuel was the same one that has powered the whole summer—AI demand showing up in real numbers.

### Cisco Raises Its AI Outlook After Amassing $9.3 Billion in Orders

The clearest read on how deep the buildout runs came from the plumbing. **Cisco Systems** projected **$7.5 billion** in revenue tied to AI data centers in fiscal 2027—about **10%** of a total revenue forecast of **$72.2 billion to $73.4 billion**—after amassing **$9.3 billion** in hyperscale AI-infrastructure orders during fiscal 2026, roughly **4.5 times** the prior year's haul, per Bloomberg and Cisco's earnings materials. Fourth-quarter revenue rose **18%** year over year to **$17.3 billion**, with non-GAAP EPS up **23%** to **$1.22**, and full-year revenue reached **$63.3 billion**, up **12%**.

The nuance is in the gap between the two numbers. Some investors were disappointed that the **$7.5 billion** revenue guide trailed the **$9.3 billion** order book, a reminder that networking gear converts backlog to revenue on a lag rather than all at once. But the direction is unmistakable: the world's largest networking supplier is now selling AI infrastructure at a scale that materially moves its top line, and the orders keep arriving faster than they can be booked. The stock rallied sharply on the print, per the coverage—the market choosing to look at the pipeline rather than the timing lag.

### Nvidia Assembles a $500 Billion Consortium to Calm Jittery Credit Markets

If the equity side has been euphoric, the credit side has been uneasy—and on Tuesday **Nvidia** moved to quiet it. CEO **Jensen Huang** signed up six financial heavyweights—**Apollo**, **BlackRock**, **Blackstone**, **Brookfield**, **Goldman Sachs**, and **KKR**—to build compute-financing platforms aimed at mobilizing more than **$500 billion** in third-party capital for the AI-infrastructure rollout, per Bloomberg. The structure is deliberately arm's-length: each firm independently judges individual deals and its own level of participation, while Nvidia's own contribution stays relatively limited and factors into only some transactions.

The point was to address a specific worry. Lenders had grown anxious about the sheer size of AI financing deals and their increasingly **circular** nature—chipmakers, clouds, and model labs funding one another in ways that can obscure real risk. The move showed up in the numbers: the cost of protecting Nvidia's debt for five years had nearly doubled to as much as **$82,000** annually per **$10 million** of principal by late last month, then eased back to about **$73,000** by Tuesday as the consortium news landed, per Bloomberg. It doesn't erase the circularity concern—bringing in outside capital is its own kind of dependency—but it does put institutional balance sheets between the buildout and the bond market, which for now is what the credit crowd wanted to see.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Asian chipmakers lead an emerging-market rally**: Emerging-market equities climbed as Asian semiconductor names rallied on the same upbeat AI-infrastructure results lifting US tech, with currency traders holding fire ahead of the US inflation print, per Bloomberg—evidence the AI trade's second wind is global, not just a Wall Street phenomenon.
- **The AI-financing debate isn't settled**: Even as Nvidia's consortium eased immediate credit-market nerves, Bloomberg noted lingering unease about the scale and circularity of AI funding deals—a counterweight to the equity euphoria and a sign scrutiny of how the buildout is paid for is here to stay.
- **Inflation math resets the rate path**: With headline CPI at 3.4% and core at 2.5%, markets moved to price a September Fed hold as the base case, per CNBC and The Motley Fool—removing, at least for now, the rate risk that had hung over the summer's tech rally.

## GNSS News

### Frequency Electronics Wins a Follow-On Order for Atomic Clocks Bound for the Moon

The most forward-looking positioning story this week points at the Moon. **Frequency Electronics** was awarded a follow-on production contract worth roughly **$8 million** to supply compact, high-precision **atomic clocks** supporting position, navigation, and timing for lunar missions, per Inside GNSS and the company's release. The award arrived just about four months after FEI's initial lunar-PNT order—an approximately **$7 million** contract announced in March 2026—with President and CEO **Tom McClelland** framing the quick repeat as evidence of the company's growing foothold in space exploration.

The significance is architectural. Building a navigation and timing layer around the Moon—the kind of lunar equivalent to GPS that agencies and contractors have been sketching for cislunar operations—depends on stable, space-qualified clocks as its foundation, exactly as terrestrial GNSS does. A follow-on production order (rather than another study or prototype) signals that at least one lunar-PNT effort is moving from design into hardware, and that the timing backbone is being ordered in quantity. Where most of this year's PNT news has been about defending Earth's signals, this is about laying the first timing infrastructure for a place that has none.

### Septentrio Shrinks Jam-Resistant Precise Positioning Into a 2-Gram Module

Back on Earth, the trend is miniaturization. **Septentrio**, now part of **Hexagon**, has extended its mosaic family with the **mosaic-G5 P6**, a multi-frequency precise-positioning module that measures just **23 mm by 16 mm** and weighs as little as **2.2 grams**—small and power-frugal enough for commercial UAVs, robots, and other size-constrained platforms, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. Despite the footprint, it ships with the company's **AIM+ Premium** anti-jamming and anti-spoofing technology and is compatible with the **Galileo High Accuracy Service** for decimeter-level positioning out of the box.

The design choices map neatly onto where autonomy is headed. The module pairs one of the higher update rates on the market with low latency—useful for tight control loops—and supports single- or dual-antenna configurations to deliver GNSS heading alongside pitch or roll, the attitude data an autonomous vehicle needs to know not just where it is but which way it's pointed. The subtext echoes a theme that has run through the year: resilient, jam-resistant positioning only matters at scale if it can be made small, cheap, and embeddable. Packing spoofing protection and decimeter accuracy into a two-gram part is a bet that the drones and robots multiplying fastest are exactly the ones that can least afford to be fooled.
