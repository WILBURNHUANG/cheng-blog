Friday's news kept tracing the same fault line: the contest between the United States and China over artificial intelligence, fought on three fronts at once. The first was supply, where Taiwan moved to put criminal weight behind the chip-export curbs Washington has been pressing for. The second was capability, where Microsoft finished training a giant base model aimed squarely at the open-weight systems coming out of Chinese labs. The third was power, where one of the country's biggest LNG exporters said it would build its own gas plants rather than wait for the grid. In positioning, the month's steady lesson held once more—harden navigation against a lost or lying signal—heard this time from a commercial module maker and from government auditors warning the military's own backup is not ready.

## Tech News

### Taiwan Moves to Make Unauthorized AI-Chip Exports to China a Crime

The day's defining story put teeth into the chip-control regime. **Taiwan** is preparing much stricter export rules on advanced AI chips and servers bound for **China**, including criminal penalties under its **Foreign Trade Act** to prosecute smuggling and diversion rings, per Bloomberg and a brief from OODA Loop dated **October 16**. The move stems from broader coordination with Washington, including commitments under the new **US–Taiwan trade pact**, and follows investigations into black-market shipments of high-performance **Nvidia** and **AMD** hardware routed into the mainland through regional intermediaries.

The significance is that the island at the center of the world's advanced-chip supply chain is now aligning its own enforcement with US policy rather than just its manufacturing. Criminalizing diversion raises the stakes for the gray-market networks that have moved restricted silicon despite existing controls, and signals that the choke point on China's AI ambitions is tightening from the production side, not only the design side. The measures were described as upcoming, with no effective date yet set and no specific fines or sentences disclosed—so the scope will matter as much as the intent once the statute is written.

### Microsoft Finishes Pre-Training MAI-Base-2, Its Answer to Open-Weight Rivals

The clearest capability story came from Microsoft's in-house model team. **Microsoft AI** said on **October 15** it had completed pre-training of **MAI-Base-2**, a mixture-of-experts model with **2.8 trillion** total parameters and **80 billion** active, now in post-training, per a Microsoft AI technical post. The company trained it natively on vision using an encoder-free architecture, on **16,000 GB200** GPUs over **38 days** across August and September, and claims it is **4.2 to 12.3x** more pre-training-FLOPs-efficient than **DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base** on held-out evaluations.

The strategic point is in how it will ship and who it targets. "MAI-Base-2 outperforms every open-weight model we tested on our held-out evaluations," Microsoft wrote, naming DeepSeek, **Kimi K3**, and **Qwen** checkpoints as the comparison set—then said it plans to **license the weights to trusted partners through Foundry** rather than release them openly. After a year of leaning on OpenAI, it is Microsoft's strongest signal yet that it intends to field frontier base models of its own, pitched explicitly against the Chinese open-weight systems that have reset the field's price-performance expectations.

### Venture Global Reserves Up to 10 GW of Off-Grid Gas Power for AI Data Centers

The day's biggest infrastructure bet was about escaping the grid entirely. **Venture Global**, the US LNG exporter, said on **October 16** it had signed new agreements with **GE Vernova** and **Baker Hughes** to reserve up to **16** gas-turbine "power islands"—each rated around **720 megawatts**—to support more than **10 gigawatts** of grid-independent power for AI data centers by **2034**, per the company and coverage from Oilprice and Benzinga. The turbines are designed to do double duty, able to run the company's LNG facilities or feed dedicated compute campuses.

The move matters because electricity, not chips, is increasingly the binding constraint on the AI buildout, and grid interconnection queues can stretch for years. By pre-ordering generation capacity and building behind-the-meter, Venture Global is betting that hyperscalers will pay a premium for power they can switch on without waiting for utilities—turning a gas-export company into an AI-infrastructure supplier, and adding to the wave of capital flowing into the unglamorous base of the stack.

## GNSS News

### Xsens Puts a Hardened Quad-Band GNSS/INS Module Into Mass Distribution

The freshest positioning news was about getting resilient, high-accuracy navigation into the hands of system builders. **Xsens** said on **October 15** that its **Sirius** RTK GNSS/INS modules are now stocked by distributor **Mouser Electronics**, pairing a **quad-band** GNSS receiver with inertial sensor fusion to hold centimeter-level positioning and stable orientation "where conventional GNSS/INS systems degrade," per a release carried by Military+Aerospace Electronics. The modules expose RS232, RS422, CAN, and CAN-FD interfaces, output a real-time uncertainty estimate so a host can judge how much to trust the fix, and are **MIL-STD-202** tested for shock, vibration, and environmental robustness.

The move matters because resilient positioning only scales when it is easy to design in. By routing an INS-backed, multi-band receiver through a mass distributor rather than a defense-procurement channel, Xsens is targeting autonomous ground vehicles, robotics, marine surface and subsea platforms, surveying, and mapping—applications that need to keep navigating when the satellite signal is jammed, reflected, or briefly lost. The built-in uncertainty output echoes a theme running through this month's GNSS news: a receiver that can say how much to trust itself is worth more in contested conditions than one that is merely precise.

### GAO Warns the Pentagon's GPS Backup Is Fragmented and Behind Schedule

The complementary story was about the gap on the government side of the same problem. A new **Government Accountability Office** report, dated **October 7** and circulated by USNI News on **October 12**, found that the Defense Department's reliance on GPS—"vulnerable to interference and jamming"—is outrunning its backup plans, with GPS modernization behind schedule and over budget. In 2026 the Pentagon **canceled** the long-delayed **Next-Generation Operational Control System (OCX)** and shifted to modernizing the existing control segment instead, a change GAO says creates risk for launching and operating new satellites.

The deeper finding was about fragmentation. Progress on **M-code** receivers—the jam-resistant military GPS signal—is uneven, with the Army and Navy fielding M-code-capable and multi-PNT receivers in vehicles and ships while aviation programs still struggle with integration, and complementary efforts such as non-GPS satellite receivers and automated celestial navigation scattered across the services. GAO made **three recommendations**, one per military department, urging each to consolidate its GPS-modernization and complementary-PNT work for better alignment; the Pentagon concurred with all three. It is the civil-oversight counterpart to the week's commercial resilience push: the hardware to survive a denied signal is arriving faster in industry than the programs meant to field it across the force.
