Monday's tech news kept circling the same two subjects: the money piling into AI and the question of who governs it. Anthropic moved to set aside a slice of its own equity for the public even as it edged toward one of the largest IPOs ever contemplated, a chip startup aimed at Nvidia drew a megaround led by some of tech's biggest names, and Washington drew another line around a Chinese model. Under the headlines ran a quieter current of worry—about how much debt the buildout is taking on, and about the first signs of autonomous agents being turned to harm. In positioning, the month's steady lesson held once more: build navigation that keeps working when the satellite signal is jammed, spoofed, or gone.

## Tech News

### Anthropic Earmarks 5% of Its Equity for a Public-Benefit Fund as a $1.5 Trillion IPO Looms

The day's defining story put a number on how AI's winners might share the upside. **Anthropic** said it would pledge about **5%** of its shares—worth roughly **$48 billion** at its last reported valuation—to a new public-benefit fund, subject to shareholder approval, per the company and coverage from Tech Startups and AI Insider. The structure would add **2%** more for every **50%** rise in the company's value above an undisclosed baseline; combined with existing founder and employee commitments, Anthropic says about **20%** of its shares would eventually serve public-benefit purposes through a wholly owned LLC making nonprofit donations or direct distributions.

The context is the scale of what Anthropic is becoming. It was valued at roughly **$965 billion** in a May round, according to the reporting, and may pursue a **November IPO** near **$1.5 trillion**—a figure that would place it among the most valuable companies ever to go public. Setting aside equity for public benefit before such an offering is both a governance statement and a signal of how much paper wealth the frontier-lab boom has created. The pledge echoes Elon Musk's separate promise of one **SpaceX** share per American child, worth about **$10.5 billion**—two gestures that underscore how concentrated, and how contested, AI's value has become.

### Tenstorrent Raises $1.5 Billion at a $6.2 Billion Valuation to Chase Nvidia

The clearest funding story came from the silicon challengers. **Tenstorrent**, the **RISC-V** AI-chip startup led by veteran architect **Jim Keller**, raised **$1.5 billion** at a **$6.2 billion** valuation—more than double its December 2024 mark—per Reuters and Tech Startups. The company said the money will expand production of its **Galaxy** systems, which package its processors into rack-scale machines pitched as an open, royalty-free alternative to Nvidia's accelerators and the proprietary stacks around them.

The significance is where investors are placing their bets as the AI-chip field widens. Keller has built a career designing processors at AMD, Apple, and Tesla, and Tenstorrent's RISC-V approach promises customers freedom from licensing fees and vendor lock-in. The round lands amid a run of mega-raises in the compute layer and a broader debate over whether the buildout can earn a return—money flowing to a credible Nvidia rival is a vote that the accelerator market will be large and plural enough to support more than one architecture.

### DHS Bans DeepSeek From Civilian Federal IT

Washington drew another boundary around Chinese AI. The **Department of Homeland Security** ordered civilian federal agencies to remove **DeepSeek** products and any third-party services built on its models, and barred future procurement, per Reuters. The directive was issued under the **Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018**, the same supply-chain authority used to push other foreign technology out of government systems.

The move matters because it treats an open-weight model—not just a device or a vendor—as a supply-chain risk to be excised from federal networks. DeepSeek's low-cost systems have reset price-performance expectations across the industry and shown up repeatedly as the benchmark rivals measure themselves against, which makes a government-wide ban as much a statement about data and provenance as about capability. It extends a pattern of U.S. policy aimed at walling off Chinese AI from sensitive deployments while the competition over models and chips intensifies.

## GNSS News

### Elsight Puts Cellular-Based Navigation Into Drones for GPS-Denied Flight

The freshest positioning news was about navigating when the satellites go silent. **Elsight**, a provider of mission-critical connectivity for uncrewed systems, unveiled a positioning capability built for **GPS-denied, jammed, and spoofed** environments across air, land, and maritime platforms, per GPS World (October 14). Fully integrated into the company's flagship **Halo** product, it uses a patented cellular approach—fusing network **Timing Advance**, surrounding base-station signals, verified GPS, and motion vectors—to deliver absolute geolocation without GPS, internet, visual reference, or even a communications link.

The move matters because it adds a distinct, independent layer to resilient navigation rather than another flavor of satellite correction. GPS is used by default and the cellular engine takes over when it is unavailable, while an active spoofing-detection function compares the two position sources in real time and raises an alert when they diverge. For existing Halo customers the capability ships as a software unlock with no new hardware; Elsight says Halo has logged more than **500,000** operational hours across **100-plus** customers in high-intensity combat settings and sits on the DCMA Blue List. No accuracy figures were disclosed—the pitch is availability and trust, not centimeters.

### NAL Technologies Ships a Plug-In Anti-Jam Upgrade for Fielded GNSS Gear

The complementary story was about hardening equipment already in the field. **NAL Technologies (Naltec)** launched **PntGuard Syntrx**, a smart-antenna upgrade that protects GNSS timing and location data in existing devices without hardware replacement or costly re-engineering, per Inside GNSS (October 9). The plug-and-play unit automatically detects interference and dynamically adjusts its output so that connected equipment keeps running and retains both timing and situational awareness.

The significance is in the delivery model. Much of the critical infrastructure that depends on GPS timing—and much of the fleet of receivers already deployed—cannot be swapped out quickly or cheaply, so an upgrade that drops in front of existing gear lowers the bar to resilience. It fits the month's running theme on the commercial side of GNSS: the fastest path to surviving a denied or deceptive signal is often retrofitting what is already installed, not waiting for a new generation of hardware.
