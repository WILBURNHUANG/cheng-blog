Two days after the midterm sweep sent the Dow to a record, the market took it all back and then some. Thursday was a rout: the AI trade that has carried the indexes all year unwound in a single session, and this time there was no megacap to hide behind—every one of the S&P 500's eleven sectors closed lower, the volatility gauge doubled, and the names that had minted the year's gains gave them back fastest. The trigger was the same policy shift investors had cheered on Tuesday, now read the other way: a Democratic Congress that might finally rein in the data-center buildout. In positioning, the week's resilience theme moved to Europe, where the continent ordered more of its own navigation satellites and paid a cluster of Norwegian startups to keep ships and drones moving when the signals go dark.

## Tech News

### The AI Trade Unwinds in the Worst Session Since the 2025 Tariff Shock

Thursday erased the election rally and more. The **Nasdaq Composite** plunged **5.52%** to **24,315.00**, falling more than **10%** from its recent highs into correction territory, while the **S&P 500** dropped **4.36%** to **7,353.73** and the **Dow Jones Industrial Average** sank **3.44%**—**1,833 points**—to **51,523.06**, per market data aggregated by TechFlow. The tech-heavy **Nasdaq-100** cratered **6.36%** to **26,629.81** and the **Russell 2000** lost **4.45%** to **2,729.89**. All three major indexes logged their largest single-day percentage declines since the **April 4, 2025** tariff shock, and all **eleven S&P 500 sectors** closed lower, led down by information technology at **-7.30%**.

The fear showed up in the plumbing. The **Cboe Volatility Index** jumped **11.57 points** to **28.18**, its highest reading since March and its biggest one-day gain since that same April 2025 session. The catalyst, again, was Washington: investors who had spent Tuesday betting a Democratic sweep would "save AI spenders from themselves" spent Thursday pricing in the downside of that same bet—regulatory risk hanging over the data-center boom, with worries about new AI rules compounding the selloff in the most capital-intensive names.

### More Than $1 Trillion Evaporates From the Magnificent Seven

The damage concentrated exactly where the gains had. The **Magnificent Seven** collectively shed more than **$1 trillion** in market value in a single day. **Nvidia** led the destruction, falling more than **9%** and erasing over **$440 billion**—enough to flip its year-to-date performance from a gain into a loss of roughly **2.6%**. **Apple** and **Microsoft**, which had set record highs on Wednesday, both pulled back.

The broader chip complex fared even worse. **AMD** and **Micron** each dropped about **17%**, memory maker **SanDisk** fell as much as **22%** intraday, and AI-cloud provider **CoreWeave** slid about **10%**. The **PHLX Semiconductor Index** tumbled roughly **12%**, its second-largest one-day decline on record, while power-infrastructure play **GE Vernova** dropped **7.5%**. It was the mirror image of the trade that had defined the year: the names most levered to AI spending, which had absorbed every dollar of enthusiasm on the way up, absorbed the full force of the repricing on the way down.

### After the Bell, the Split Tape Returns

For all the carnage in the regular session, the earnings tape stayed two-sided after hours. **Cloudflare** jumped about **14%** to **$279** on fourth-quarter guidance above estimates, **JFrog** soared roughly **25%** after raising its full-year outlook, and **Airbnb** climbed about **11%** to **$188.89** after third-quarter revenue of **$4.87 billion** beat expectations and the company added a **$12 billion** buyback.

The losers were just as sharp. **DraftKings** fell about **22%** after a third-quarter loss of **$0.94** a share on revenue of **$1.33 billion**, both missing estimates; **Atlassian** dropped more than **15%** on a slowing data-center revenue outlook; **Axon** slid about **13%** on results below expectations; and buy-now-pay-later lender **Affirm** fell roughly **11%** despite a slight guidance beat. Even on the market's worst day in eighteen months, investors kept sorting the winners from the losers one print at a time.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Jobless claims come in strong**: Initial **unemployment claims** fell to **189,000**, below the **197,000** expected, a reminder that the labor market was not the source of Thursday's panic.
- **Treasuries catch a bid**: The **10-year Treasury yield** slipped more than **5 basis points** to **4.839%** as money fled equities for the safety of government debt.
- **Oil firms as stocks fall**: **WTI crude** rose **2.7%** to **$90.52** and **Brent** gained **3.8%** to **$98.29**, bucking the risk-off tone in equities.
- **Bitcoin slides with risk assets**: **Bitcoin** fell about **6%** to around **$75,000**, tracking the broader flight from speculative positions.
- **A rotation with nowhere to hide**: Unlike the late-October selloffs that spared defensive sectors, Thursday's decline dragged every S&P 500 sector lower, a sign the de-risking had turned indiscriminate.

## GNSS News

### ESA Orders Four More Galileo G2 Satellites, Pushing the Fleet to 16

The freshest positioning story was a bet on sovereignty. In late October, the **European Space Agency** extended its second-generation Galileo contracts with the program's two prime manufacturers—**Thales Alenia Space** in Italy and **Airbus Defence and Space** in Germany—adding **two satellites each** to the six they had held since 2021, per Inside GNSS. Signed on behalf of the **EU Agency for the Space Programme (EUSPA)**, the orders lift the **Galileo G2** fleet to **16** spacecraft, with the four new satellites due for delivery in **2031**. Integration and flight testing of the first G2 satellites begin in **2027**, and the first launch is planned for **2028**.

The language around the deal made the strategic subtext explicit. "These contracts represent an investment in Europe's ability to navigate its own future, independently and with confidence," said **Laurent Jaffart**, ESA's Director of Resilience, Navigation and Connectivity. Thales Alenia Space CEO **Hervé Derrey** called the program "a cornerstone of Europe's strategic autonomy," while **Alain Fauré**, head of space systems at Airbus Defence and Space, said the satellites are "vital to augment navigation capabilities for European security and defence." The G2 generation is designed to deliver stronger, more jam-resistant signals than today's constellation—a direct response to the interference now washing across European skies.

### ESA Pays Three Norwegian Startups to Keep Ships and Drones Moving When GNSS Fails

The complementary story came from the opposite end of the scale—not a constellation, but the scrappy terrestrial defenses around it. On **October 27** at Spaceport Norway in Oslo, ESA signed three contracts under its **Navigation Innovation and Support Programme (NAVISP)**, funded through a call from the **Norwegian Space Agency**, each targeting a different corner of the GNSS-interference problem, per Inside GNSS. The urgency is quantified: a Norwegian government analysis estimated that a seven-day loss of satellite-based positioning, navigation and timing could cost the country about **NOK 3.4 billion** (roughly **EUR 320 million**).

The three projects divide the work cleanly. **Testnor**, in Tromsø, is building a market-ready **Signal Data Library** of GNSS-interference recordings and RF playbacks, seeded with data from the Jammertest exercise it coordinates. **Norse Navigation**, in Trondheim, is developing maritime positioning sensors for SOLAS-compliant merchant ships that keep working through interference, combining multi-constellation GNSS, **Galileo OSNMA** signal authentication, jamming and spoofing detection, and degraded-mode operation with bridge-compatible alerts. And **Rift Dynamics**, in Sandnes, working with antenna maker **Comrod**, is building navigation for low-cost, expendable drones, with Arctic jamming and spoofing trials planned and a target of **ten minutes** of reliable operation in GNSS-denied conditions. "Strengthening PNT resilience is a priority for Norway," said **Kristian Svartveit**, senior advisor for satellite navigation at the Norwegian Space Agency, pointing to a recent report on PNT's role in the country's total-defence posture.
