Friday's news ran on a single current: capabilities arriving faster than the systems meant to contain them. OpenAI declared the AGI era open with a model powerful enough that it had to lock parts of it down. Tesla put driverless cars with no steering wheel on public roads and had a federal probe by lunchtime. Money kept pouring into the picks-and-shovels layer—compute, GPUs, data centers—faster than anyone can pour concrete. And in positioning, the throughline was survival when the satellite signal isn't there: a radar network holding formation on a quantum clock, and a drone finding its way home by looking at the ground.

## Tech News

### Update: OpenAI Ships GPT-6 Astra, Its First Broadly Deployed 'Critical' Cyber Model

Two days after flagging Astra's cyber risk internally, **OpenAI** launched it. **GPT-6 Astra** is now rolling out—first to a limited group of organizations, then to ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise, and to developers through the OpenAI API, **Microsoft Azure**, and **Amazon Bedrock**, per TechStartups. API pricing starts at **$10 per million input tokens** and **$50 per million output tokens**, and OpenAI is pitching it for end-to-end professional work across software engineering, computer use, browsing, science, and cybersecurity.

The genuinely new detail is the deployment posture. Astra is OpenAI's first broadly available system to cross the "Critical" cybersecurity threshold in its Preparedness Framework—meaning it can, under some conditions, find previously unknown vulnerabilities and build exploits against hardened systems without continuous human direction. That forced tighter internal isolation, encrypted checkpoints, expanded trajectory monitoring, and extra alignment reviews before shipping. The pattern the industry rehearsed in theory—gate the offensive-grade tier, ship the rest—is now a live product with a price sheet.

### U.S. Opens Tesla Cybercab Safety Probe Hours After Launch

**Tesla's** driverless future hit a regulator almost immediately. The **National Highway Traffic Safety Administration** opened a probe into nearly **1,000** Tesla **Cybercabs** shortly after the company began putting them on public roads in Austin, Texas, examining whether the vehicles meet federal motor vehicle safety standards, per Reuters. The purpose-built robotaxi has no steering wheel, no pedals, and no conventional mirrors.

The awkwardness is that Tesla self-certified the Cybercab as compliant—a normal step—but most existing standards were written for cars with human drivers. Federal officials have proposed modernizing those rules for autonomous vehicles, yet Tesla's commercial deployment is moving faster than the rulemaking. The outcome could become a test case for the whole sector, including **Waymo** and **Zoox**: if purpose-built vehicles need exemptions or new certification before scaling, the consequences reach well past Austin.

### Crusoe Triples to a $30 Billion Valuation as the Compute Land Grab Accelerates

**Crusoe** reportedly raised more than **$3 billion** at a valuation around **$30 billion**, roughly tripling its worth in under a year after a $1.38 billion round at about $10 billion just ten months ago, per Reuters. **Atreides Management** and **Valor Equity Partners** co-led, with **Mubadala Capital** joining. Crusoe started in 2018 burning stranded natural gas to mine crypto; it now supplies GPUs and data-center capacity to the likes of **Meta**, **Microsoft**, and **OpenAI**, and signed a roughly **$13 billion** five-year deal to give **Jane Street** GPU infrastructure.

The valuation is another vote that the biggest AI winners may not be the model labs at all, but the companies supplying power, cooling, networking, and silicon underneath them. "Neocloud" has gone from jargon to a category investors will pay $30 billion for.

## GNSS News

### Saab and the Royal Navy Sync a Radar Network on a Quantum Clock, Not GPS

The sharpest "lose the satellite, keep the mission" story of the week came from a radar range. **Saab UK**, quantum-timing startup **Aquark Technologies**, and the **Royal Navy's** Disruptive Capabilities and Technologies Office demonstrated that a distributed **Giraffe 1X** radar network can hold a single coherent air picture using quantum timing instead of GNSS, per Inside GNSS. In a June 2026 trial across multiple UK sites—with QinetiQ, DSTL, and the experimentation vessel **XV Patrick Blackett**—radars at separate locations tracked live targets while relying solely on **Aquark's AQlock** cold-atom clock for their timing reference.

Why timing, not position, is the point: distributed radars must agree on time to within billionths of a second to fuse their tracks, and a timing error becomes a target-location error. GNSS usually supplies that reference and is exactly what gets jammed first. When the team injected controlled timing errors to simulate spoofing, the network kept producing one air picture, degraded predictably, and recovered fast once sync returned—what Saab believes is the first such demonstration on independent quantum timing. It's the resilience conversation moving from the receiver to the clock.

### Leonardo and Telespazio Fly Drones Home by Camera When GNSS Goes Dark

On the map side, **Leonardo** and **Telespazio** completed a proof-of-concept with Italian startup **Raysilience** for backup navigation of UAVs operating without GNSS, per Inside GNSS. The "Backup Solution for GNSS-Denied Navigation" project grew out of Raysilience's Test-It win at the 2024 Telespazio Technology Contest—a team from Rome's **La Sapienza** and **LUISS Guido Carli** universities proposing drone positioning from satellite imagery and matching algorithms rather than radio signals.

The delivered system is a plug-and-play **Visual Positioning System** that estimates location by comparing what the aircraft sees to reference satellite imagery, built to drop into different platforms as a backup layer. Leonardo said all deliverables were met, validating the approach and pointing toward broader UAV use wherever positioning continuity matters. It's a cheaper, sensor-light complement to the quantum and alternative-signal work elsewhere: when the sky goes quiet, look at the ground.
