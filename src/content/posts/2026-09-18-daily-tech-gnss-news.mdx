Friday's news came down to a spreadsheet question: can the AI buildout's economics survive its own ambition? OpenAI's internal numbers, leaked to the Financial Times, put a jaw-dropping figure on the cash it plans to torch before the payoff arrives—while a major infrastructure investor argued the whole thing is already decelerating because the physical world can't keep pace. Around that debate, the tangible economy kept moving: a new iPhone shipped straight into the memory crunch, and the hyperscalers went shopping for batteries that last days, not hours. In positioning, the industry's biggest week wrapped with the same message it opened on—assume the signal will be attacked—now backed by fresh hardware and a market report that reads like a resilience manifesto.

## Tech News

### OpenAI's Own Math: $278 Billion of Cash Burn on the Way to $350 Billion in Revenue

The defining number of the day came from inside OpenAI. The company projects **negative free cash flow of about $278 billion** from 2026 through the end of 2030, according to materials the Financial Times reviewed and Bloomberg summarized—figures shared with partners as part of a major computing deal. Over the same stretch, OpenAI expects revenue to climb from roughly **$36 billion this year to $350 billion in 2030**, with spending on compute and talent designed to vastly outrun those gains until the very end of the window.

The scale is the story. A quarter-trillion-dollar cash outflow is unlike anything the technology industry has attempted, and it lands amid a week of open argument over whether the AI trade has gotten ahead of itself. The bet is coherent on its own terms—buy enough compute now to lock in a dominant position, then harvest it after 2030—but it depends on revenue that would need to grow nearly tenfold and on capital markets willing to fund years of losses first. It is, in one line item, the entire optimism-versus-gravity debate the sector has been having all month.

### Apple's iPhone 18 Pro Ships Into the Memory Shortage—$100 More Expensive

The most tangible product launch of the day was in Apple stores. The **iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max** went on sale Friday, the first iPhones released under CEO **John Ternus** and, notably, the first to reach market in the middle of the global memory and storage shortage, per Yahoo Finance's markets coverage. The squeeze shows up right on the price tag: the new Pro models cost about **$100 more** than last year's, a rare mid-cycle increase Apple tied to component costs it couldn't fully absorb.

That price move is the memory supercycle reaching the consumer's pocket. For a year the DRAM and NAND crunch has been an enterprise and data-center story; a flagship phone getting more expensive because chips are scarce is the moment it becomes visible to everyone. Tech stocks took the launch in stride—the Nasdaq rose roughly **1%** on the day—but the pricing is a quiet signal that the same shortage feeding AI accelerators is now taxing the highest-volume consumer device on earth.

### Meta and Google Buy Batteries That Last Days to Keep AI Running

The clearest sign of where the infrastructure money is flowing came from the grid. **Meta** and **Google** are investing heavily in **long-duration energy storage**—batteries that can discharge for tens of hours or even multiple days—to power AI data centers whose appetite is outstripping what the grid can reliably deliver, per Bloomberg. Google's recent deals include a partnership with **MN8 Energy** pairing solar with zinc-based long-duration storage, on top of an earlier iron-air system with **Form Energy** promising **100-hour** discharge.

The math behind the shift is straightforward: lithium-ion typically covers a few hours, but an AI campus that must stay lit through demand spikes and grid shortfalls needs something that spans days. Since the start of 2025, at least **150 gigawatt-hours** of long-duration storage has been announced, approved, or financed in the U.S.—more than five times the capacity planned at the end of 2024, with most of the new demand coming from data centers. It's a reminder that the AI race is increasingly won or lost on electrons, not just silicon.

## GNSS News

### Calian Expands Its Precision and Anti-Jam Antenna Lineup as ION GNSS+ Week Closes

The freshest hardware news of the week came from Ottawa. **Calian GNSS** expanded its precision-positioning and resilient-PNT portfolio with three new antennas: the survey-grade **Accutenna 4** pole-mount (AC4990PXF), an updated pole-mount **controlled reception pattern antenna (CRPA)** (CR8894PXF+), and the full-band **AJ979XF+-MAR** anti-jamming antenna, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The Accutenna 4 uses a four-feed compact stacked-patch design that holds a stable phase center—phase-center variation under **3 mm**—for repeatable RTK, PPK, and PPP work in the field.

The lineup is a neat snapshot of where precision positioning now lives: one antenna for accuracy, two for surviving interference. Calian aimed the release squarely at surveying, mapping, critical infrastructure, marine, autonomy, public-safety, and defense users operating in increasingly hostile RF environments—the same customers who, a few years ago, would have shopped on centimeter accuracy alone and now shop on whether the antenna keeps working while under attack. Landing during ION GNSS+ week, it underscores how thoroughly anti-jam and anti-spoof capability has migrated from a defense specialty into a mainstream commercial requirement.

### Keysight's PNT Report 2026 Maps a Market Rebuilt Around Resilience and LEO-PNT

The week also produced a market read to match the hardware. **Keysight Technologies** published its **PNT Report 2026**, describing a positioning, navigation, and timing market being reshaped by resilient architectures, **LEO-PNT**, autonomy, sovereign national systems, new threats, and increasingly demanding assurance requirements, per Inside GNSS. The report frames these not as separate trends but as one shift: the assumption of a clean, always-available satellite signal has broken, and every layer of the market is reorganizing around that fact.

What makes the report notable is the through-line it draws across a fragmented industry. Governments pursuing sovereign PNT, startups launching low-Earth-orbit navigation constellations, automakers hardening receivers, and test houses building interference into the lab are all responding to the same pressure. The document reads less like a forecast and more like a status check on a field that has spent all year proving, in one demonstration and product launch after another, that resilience—not raw accuracy—is now the axis the whole PNT market turns on.
