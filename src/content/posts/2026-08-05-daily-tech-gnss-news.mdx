Wednesday was about the moment things get real—when a demo becomes a business and a claim becomes a contract. Amazon's robotaxi arm stopped giving Las Vegas free rides and started charging for them. The market kept applying its new, unsentimental rule to the AI trade: show me the cash flow. And SpaceX, one day into life as a public company, answered skeptics not with modesty but with a bigger number. In positioning, the theme was permission granted—a startup cleared to put a rival signal next to GPS, and a jamming weapon cleared for the open ocean.

## Tech News

### Zoox Flips the Meter On: Paid Robotaxi Rides Come to Las Vegas

**Zoox**, Amazon's autonomous-vehicle unit, will begin charging passengers for driverless rides in Las Vegas on **August 10**, ending nearly a year of free public rides and marking the start of commercial operations, per TechCrunch, CNBC, and Axios. The switch to paid service follows a temporary exemption from the **National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)** that lets Zoox carry paying customers in vehicles that lack traditional human controls—no steering wheel, no pedals.

The milestone matters for who did it, not just what it is. Zoox is the first company to earn that NHTSA exemption for a robotaxi built from the ground up rather than a retrofitted passenger car, a harder engineering and regulatory path than bolting an autonomy stack onto an existing model. The company says it will price rides to compete with the "comfort" tier of conventional ride-hailing—a signal it intends to fight for everyday riders, not just novelty-seekers. After a spring of headlines questioning whether the robotaxi economics would ever close, this is the part where one operator stops experimenting and starts sending invoices.

### The Market Sorts the AI Trade Into Winners and Losers

The AI-spending debate has resolved into a single, unsentimental question, and this week the market answered it out loud: can a company's operating cash flow still cover the capital expenditure AI demands? Bloomberg's read on the divergent reception to **Microsoft** and **Meta** earnings put it plainly—the names whose cash generation kept pace with spending were rewarded, and the ones whose cash slipped were punished. Microsoft, which tied a massive capex ramp to validated Azure demand, rallied; Meta did not.

The Meta numbers show why. The company's shares fell nearly **10%** after hours—to about **$529** from a **$585** close—after free cash flow collapsed **91%** to just **$784 million**, a stark illustration of what happens when the spending line outruns the cash line, per Bloomberg and Investing.com. The concern isn't that Meta's revenue is weak (it grew **28%**); it's the widening gap between the money going into AI infrastructure and the money coming back out, and the long runway before those investments pay off. Set against Microsoft's reception, it's the clearest sign yet that "just spend on AI" is no longer a free pass—investors now want the buildout financed by the business, not by faith.

### Update: SpaceX Answers Its Skeptics With a Bigger Number

A day after its debut quarter landed to a lukewarm market, **SpaceX** used its first earnings call to reframe the story around growth rather than spending. CEO **Elon Musk** guided analysts to a **$100 billion annual revenue run-rate by December** and pulled the company's **$1 trillion revenue target forward to 2030**—a year ahead of prior guidance—while promising a sub-one-year payback on new AI-compute capital, per Fortune, The Motley Fool, and Calcalist. The pitch didn't stop the bleeding: the stock kept sliding on capex worries, and Fortune noted the added wrinkle that insider lockups are beginning to lift, letting early holders sell into the weakness.

The fresh detail is in the business mix behind the bravado. **Starlink** revenue rose **66%** to **$4.3 billion** as the subscriber base doubled to **12 million**, with **1.7 million** net adds in the quarter, and the company said it plans to launch at least **1,000** next-generation V3 satellites within a year. The AI line—cloud-hosting deals with **Google** and **Anthropic**, plus Grok and X advertising—grew **247%** to **$2.6 billion**. It's an aggressive answer to a simple market question: whether blistering top-line growth can outrun an eye-watering spending line long enough to matter.

## GNSS News

### The FCC Clears Xona to Put a Commercial Signal Next to GPS

In a first-of-its-kind decision, the **Federal Communications Commission** has authorized **Xona Space Systems** to broadcast **Pulsar**—the world's first commercial navigation signal designed to operate alongside GPS—from its full planned constellation of more than **250 low-Earth-orbit satellites**, per Inside GNSS. The clearance expands an earlier license covering only Xona's Pulsar-0 demonstration satellite, and it makes Xona the only FCC-authorized private navigation constellation permitted to operate next to the nation-state systems run by the U.S., Europe, China, and Russia. The company plans to launch its first six production satellites in **October** and is scaling manufacturing at its Burlingame, California facility.

The engineering choice behind the approval is the interesting part. Rather than duck the coordination problem by building in an unestablished frequency band, Xona designed Pulsar for the crowded **L-band** spectrum already used by more than **six billion** GNSS-enabled devices—signals up to **100 times stronger** than GPS and protected against spoofing by a dynamic cryptographic watermark, deliverable to many existing receivers through software and firmware updates. To win the license, Xona had to prove to the FCC, aviation stakeholders, and international governments that a much stronger new signal wouldn't step on the services already in the band. Since June 2025, Pulsar-0 has flown more than **350 transmission passes** across four continents and been independently tracked by more than a dozen commercial receivers. It's a milestone the whole year has been building toward: LEO PNT moving from promising alternative to regulator-blessed infrastructure sitting right beside GPS.

### 'Hammer of the Gods' Goes to Sea

On the military side, the U.S. **Army, Navy, and Space Force** put a maritime version of an electronic-warfare system nicknamed **Hammer of the Gods** to sea for the first time during **Project Convergence Capstone 6**, the Army's large-scale joint experiment on the West Coast in July, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. Despite the fearsome name, it isn't a conventional weapon—it's a highly deployable emitter designed to broadcast electromagnetic signals with a deliberately low signature, built to disrupt the positioning, navigation, and timing that adversary drones, precision-guided munitions, and communications all quietly depend on.

Land-based versions have shown up in exercises over the past two years, but taking the system into open water is the new step, with U.S. Space Forces Pacific and Naval Base San Diego supporting the integration. The test validated the three phases any deployable capability has to prove: getting the system into the fight, running it in a real maritime environment, and getting it back out. It fits this column's running theme from the offensive side—after months of coverage about hardening one's own PNT against jamming, this is the counterpart discipline, industrializing the ability to deny an opponent theirs, now extended from land to the contested spaces at sea.
