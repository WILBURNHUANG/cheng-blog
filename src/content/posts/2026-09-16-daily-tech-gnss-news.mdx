Wednesday was a day about the price of things—money, compute, and trust. The Federal Reserve did what markets had braced for and lifted interest rates, a reminder that the macro backdrop for richly valued tech is tightening, not loosening. And still the AI machine kept spending and shipping: a memory maker posted numbers that would have looked like typos two years ago, and Salesforce stood on stage to argue the graphical interface itself is on its way out. Underneath the noise, the AI industry's leaders spent the week arguing about how fast is too fast. In positioning, the through-line was the one that never changes lately—assume someone is trying to deny your signal—and the answers came from an Arctic test range, a 5G network, and a Pentagon program picking itself back up off the floor.

## Tech News

### The Fed Raises Rates to 3.75%-4%, Its First Hike Since 2023

The biggest macro event of the day landed at 2 p.m. The **Federal Reserve** raised its benchmark interest rate by **25 basis points** to a target range of **3.75%-4%**, its first increase since 2023, with the FOMC approving the move unanimously, per CNBC. Updated projections showed **16 of 18** participants expect at least one more hike before year-end, with no cuts penciled in until 2028—a decidedly hawkish dot plot that reset expectations for anyone hoping the central bank was near the end of its tightening.

For tech, the read-through is uncomfortable but familiar. Higher-for-longer rates raise the discount rate applied to every future dollar of profit, and the companies with the most profit pushed furthest into the future—AI infrastructure names, unprofitable software, anything trading on a multiple rather than earnings—feel it first. It follows a week in which the 10-year Treasury yield had already cracked 5%; Wednesday's decision made the direction official. The irony of the moment is hard to miss: the sector generating the most economic excitement is also the most exposed to the one variable it can't engineer around.

### Micron's Numbers Break the Scale: $41.46 Billion in Revenue, $50 Billion Guided

If the Fed set the mood, **Micron** set the counterpoint. The memory maker reported quarterly revenue of **$41.46 billion**, up a staggering **~346%** year over year and beating consensus by more than 17%, with non-GAAP EPS of **$25.11** against a **$20.28** estimate, per TradingKey's summary of the results. Data-center revenue alone topped **$25 billion** in the quarter—an annualized run rate above **$100 billion**—and the company guided next quarter to a record **$50 billion**, plus or minus $1 billion, at roughly **86% gross margin**.

The engine is high-bandwidth memory. Micron said it has locked in **price and volume for its entire calendar-2026 HBM supply**, including its leading-edge **HBM4**, and it sees the HBM total addressable market growing from about $35 billion in 2025 to roughly **$100 billion by 2028**. To feed the demand it is building **Micron Research Labs**, a **$10 billion** AI-focused research facility in Boise, Idaho. Coming the same day the Fed warned on rates, the report was a blunt statement that, at least in the plumbing of AI, demand is still outrunning supply by a wide margin.

### Salesforce Declares 'AI Replaces the UI' at Dreamforce as Altman Asks for Trust

Down the coast in San Francisco, **Salesforce** used its Dreamforce keynote to make a bold claim about software's future: the user interface itself is going away. The company launched **AIforce**, framing it around the idea that AI—not menus, buttons, and forms—will become the primary way people interact with business software, per Salesforce Ben's coverage. It's an aggressive bet from a company whose fortune was built on the CRM screens it now says are on borrowed time.

The event doubled as a stage for the industry's safety debate. Fresh off a week of warnings from AI researchers and executives that the technology carries existential risk, **OpenAI's Sam Altman** offered a toned-down message—"We will get it right… I am very confident in our company's ability, our industry's ability, to do this safely," he said, per the San Francisco Standard—while **Nvidia's Jensen Huang** and **Anthropic's Dario Amodei** took their own turns before a crowd of tens of thousands. The subtext was the same tension running through the whole sector: the people building the most powerful models are also the ones asking to be trusted with them.

## GNSS News

### Jammertest Returns to Andøya: 127 Organizations Gather to Break GPS on Purpose

The world's largest open positioning-resilience exercise is back. **Jammertest 2026** runs **September 14–18** on the island of **Andøya**, Norway—about 300 kilometers north of the Arctic Circle—for the fifth consecutive year, drawing **127 organizations** and more than **300 participants** to a range where engineers deliberately jam and spoof satellite signals to see how real equipment holds up, per GIM International and Norwegian regulator Nkom. Past editions have produced eye-opening results, including a demonstration in which operators spent 40 minutes convincing a helicopter's GPS it was flying into the sea, as reported by TechSpot.

The exercise has grown from a niche curiosity into a fixture precisely because the threat has gone mainstream. With satellite interference intensifying around Russia's war in Ukraine—and spilling into civil aviation across the Baltic and Eastern Europe—Jammertest gives manufacturers a controlled but realistic place to prove their receivers, timing systems, and autonomous platforms can survive an environment an adversary controls. That so many commercial firms now send teams to a remote Arctic island to have their GPS deliberately defeated is itself a signal of where the industry's priorities have moved.

### NextNav's 5G-Powered 3D PNT Closes to Within a Meter of GPS

While Jammertest stress-tested existing kit, one company pushed a GPS alternative closer to prime time. **NextNav** said its **5G-powered 3D PNT** system achieved single-digit-meter positioning in outdoor field testing—landing within **one meter of GPS** accuracy—using **5G Positioning Reference Signals** rather than satellites, per Via Satellite's September 15 report. The company is pursuing a terrestrial network that could complement and back up GPS, a pitch that has gained urgency as jamming and spoofing incidents multiply.

The appeal of a 5G-based approach is that it leans on infrastructure already being built for other reasons, and its ground-based signals are far harder to overpower from orbit or a truck-mounted jammer than a faint satellite broadcast arriving from 20,000 kilometers up. Getting terrestrial PNT to within a meter of GPS outdoors is a meaningful milestone; the open questions remain coverage, indoor performance, and whether carriers and device makers will fold the capability into networks and chips at scale.

**Additional Headlines:**

- **Space Force charts GPS Gen4 out of the OCX wreckage**: Five months after the Space Force terminated the troubled **OCX** ground-control program—a **$6.27 billion** effort that ballooned past $8 billion before failing integration testing—it is resetting its acquisition approach around **GPS Gen4**, per Tech Times. In the interim, **Lockheed Martin** won **$114 million** in task orders (a **$96 million** award to upgrade the current ground system for **GPS IIIF** payloads and **$17.5 million** for a first-phase tech refresh), per GPS World.

## Key Takeaways

- **The macro turned against tech even as AI demand surged**: The Fed's first rate hike since 2023—to 3.75%-4%, with more signaled—raised the bar for every future-weighted valuation, yet Micron's record $41.46 billion quarter and $50 billion guide showed the AI buildout is still outrunning supply where it matters most, in memory.
- **Salesforce bet against its own interface while the safety debate raged**: Dreamforce's launch of AIforce framed AI as the successor to the graphical UI, and Altman, Huang, and Amodei used the same stage to argue over how safely and how fast the industry should move.
- **GNSS resilience advanced on three fronts at once**: 127 organizations gathered at Norway's Jammertest to defeat GPS on purpose, NextNav's 5G PNT closed to within a meter of GPS without satellites, and the Space Force began rebuilding its GPS ground-segment roadmap after the $6.27 billion OCX collapse.
