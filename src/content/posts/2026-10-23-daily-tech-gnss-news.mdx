Thursday's news was AI spending anxiety running headlong into the earnings tape. The quarter's biggest names reported strong numbers and investors sold them anyway—what spooked the market was not demand but the size of the bills being run up to chase it. Meta and Tesla both beat on key lines yet triggered a worldwide selloff, with the pain spreading from Silicon Valley to Seoul and Hong Kong, while oil climbed on the Iran war as a reminder that the macro backdrop has its own problems. Beneath the spending debate ran the week's quieter counterweight: old-line industrials cashing in on the buildout. In positioning, the month's resilience theme shifted from building hardened receivers to *seeing* the threat—characterizing interference from the ground and catching a spoofed signal with a second, independent source of truth.

## Tech News

### AI Stocks Drag Markets Down Worldwide as Spending Fears Overtake Strong Earnings

The day's defining story was a selloff that strong results couldn't stop. The **Nasdaq Composite** dropped **2.6%** to **26,008.86** and the **S&P 500** fell **1.2%** to **7,663.43**—its worst day since July—as heavy AI-infrastructure spending plans from **Meta** and **Tesla** revived investor doubts about whether the returns will ever justify the outlays, per Bloomberg. The damage was concentrated and severe: **Lam Research** fell **11.8%** *despite* a stronger-than-expected quarter, **CoreWeave** slid **10.1%** on debt concerns, **Meta** dropped **7%**, and **Nvidia**—the largest single drag on the S&P 500—lost **4.4%**.

The rout was global. South Korea's **Kospi** fell **3.5%**, Hong Kong's **Hang Seng** dropped **3%**, and Japan's **Nikkei 225** sank **2.7%**, while most individual S&P 500 names actually rose—a sign that a handful of mega-cap tech losses were overwhelming the broader market. Investors fled to safety, pushing the 10-year Treasury yield down to **4.97%** from **5.13%**. Compounding the mood, **Brent crude** jumped **4%** to **$113.17** a barrel, its highest in about five months and up from under $97 at the start of the month, as uncertainty over the war with Iran kept a risk premium on energy. After a month of bubble warnings, this was the market acting on them.

### Meta Beats on Revenue but Adds a Nonvoting Class C Share Class to Fund a $145 Billion AI Push

The clearest spark for the selloff came from Menlo Park. **Meta** reported third-quarter revenue up **26%** to **$64.6 billion**, ahead of the **$63.3 billion** estimate, with EPS of **$3.83** versus a **$3.57** forecast—and its stock fell anyway, because the story investors fixated on was the spending, per Bloomberg and Dealroom. Quarterly capital expenditure hit **$34.91 billion**, free cash flow swung to a **$2.12 billion** deficit (from **$10.63 billion** positive a year earlier), and the company narrowed its 2026 capex plan to a floor of **$135 billion**, up to **$145 billion**.

To pay for it without taking on debt or ceding control, Meta is creating a new **nonvoting Class C** common stock, distributed one-for-one to existing holders as a dividend—effectively a two-for-one split—with trading set to begin in **January 2027**. The shares give holders economic exposure but no vote, leaving CEO **Mark Zuckerberg**'s control untouched; critics argue nonvoting stock insulates management from scrutiny. For a company funding one of the largest infrastructure bets in corporate history, the structure is a tell: Meta wants a currency it can spend freely on AI while keeping the founder firmly in charge.

### Honeywell Technologies Rides the Data-Center Boom in Its First Post-Spinoff Quarter

The week's counterweight to the spending angst came from an industrial name profiting from the buildout rather than paying for it. In its first earnings report since spinning off its aerospace business in June, **Honeywell Technologies** posted net sales of about **$5 billion** (up **1%**), operating income up **79%** to **$740 million**, and segment profit up **23%** to **$1.1 billion**, with organic sales growing **7%**—above the high end of targets set in June, per the company and Manufacturing Dive. Net income fell **64%** to **$664 million**, reflecting the aerospace separation.

The AI tie-in was unmistakable: orders in the **data-center vertical were up over 100%**, and large energy projects across North America, the Middle East, Africa, and Asia have generated more than **$1 billion** in process-technology orders since the first quarter. CEO **Vimal Kapur** raised full-year guidance, lifting the sales outlook to **$20 billion–$20.1 billion** and planning to cut gross debt from **$18 billion** to under **$13 billion** by year-end. Where the chipmakers and hyperscalers absorb the capex risk, Honeywell books the automation, catalyst, and lifecycle orders the data centers generate—a reminder that the buildout pulls durable demand through to the companies that equip it.

## GNSS News

### Ground-Station Data Maps How Russia's Jamming Is Reshaping the Baltic Signal Environment

The freshest positioning story showed that resilience starts with measurement: you cannot defend against interference you cannot see. A study presented at **ION GNSS+ 2026** by **Johan Juul** of **Tallinn University of Technology** analyzed about **10 terabytes** of civilian reference-station (CORS) data from **Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania** collected from 2023 through 2025, using an LSTM autoencoder to flag anomalies and trace how Russian GNSS interference appears from the ground, per Inside GNSS (**October 21**). The picture is of a threat that has hardened over time.

The numbers are stark. At Estonia's **NJO1** station near Kingissepp/Ust-Luga, the carrier-to-noise baseline collapsed from **48–50 dB-Hz** to **30–35 dB-Hz**, with disruption escalating from episodic in 2024 to sustained denial in 2025—**51 complete outages** and **130 depression events** logged that year. During an August 2025 Ukrainian drone strike on the Ust-Luga gas complex, NJO1 saw **97.7% receiver denial** over 72 hours with both L1 and L2 suppressed, and a March 2026 strike at Auvere coincided with a synchronized nine-hour blackout. Lithuania's stations, farther from the sources, held a steadier **43–44 dB-Hz** baseline with hours-long rather than days-long outages. Juul's broader point is that ground monitoring can characterize interference that aviation and maritime reports alone miss—and that in this region, jamming has been a daily reality since 2024.

### Finnish Researchers Fuse AIS With GNSS to Catch Spoofing in a Simulated Fairway

The complementary story attacked the detection problem at sea with sensor fusion. **Heidi Kuusniemi** of **Tampere University** and the **Finnish Geospatial Research Institute** presented preliminary work at ION GNSS+ 2026 on using **AIS** vessel-motion data to back up GNSS integrity monitoring, after Finnish authorities logged **392 vessels** reporting interference in Finnish waters between January and August 2026—a count she believes understates the real total, especially among recreational boaters, per Inside GNSS (**October 19**). The question the framework answers is operational: can a vessel trust its position relative to the navigable width of a tight channel?

The method pairs two checks—a chi-square **RAIM** test of GNSS internal consistency and an **AIS dead-reckoning** check that predicts motion from reported position, speed, and course—and fuses them over time with a two-state hidden Markov model that filters out isolated glitches. In a simulated Helsinki fairway (10 vessels, a 300-meter channel, updates every two seconds), GNSS-only RAIM caught about **75%** of jamming but only **46%** of spoofing epochs; AIS dead reckoning caught **99.5%** of spoofing; and the fused model hit **93%** of jamming and **99.5%** of spoofing with false alarms below **1%**. The honest caveat is that AIS usually derives from GNSS too, so true independence would require a separate positioning source—Kuusniemi stressed the work is "preliminary." Still, it echoes the month's running lesson from a new angle: survivability comes from a second source that can catch the first one lying.
