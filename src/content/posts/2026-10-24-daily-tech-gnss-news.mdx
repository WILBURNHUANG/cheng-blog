Friday's news was a market trying to find its footing after a brutal Thursday. The indexes did not snap back so much as sort themselves out: chipmakers steadied, a defensive bid lifted cybersecurity and software, and the Dow stayed underwater as oil pushed to fresh multi-month highs on the Iran war. The day's loudest corporate story had nothing to do with earnings—it was a report that SpaceX wants to rent its way into the wireless business on tens of thousands of existing towers, a move that would reorder an industry rather than join it. Beneath the market noise, the quarter's earnings kept confirming that the AI buildout is pulling durable demand through to storage, energy, and oilfield services. In positioning, the month's resilience theme widened past the receiver to the two ends of the problem: a new satellite that turns GNSS signals into weather data, and a military system built to contest the signal environment itself.

## Tech News

### Markets Steady Unevenly as a Software Rally Offsets Oil's Drag on the Dow

After Thursday's rout, Friday was less a rebound than a rotation. The **Nasdaq Composite** finished nearly flat, down **0.21%** to **25,955.34**, and the **S&P 500** eased **0.52%** to **7,623.68**, while the **Dow Jones Industrial Average** fell **1.23%**—more than **600 points**—to **52,215.16**, per TradingKey. The split tells the story: the **Philadelphia Semiconductor Index** actually rose **0.44%** to **10,779.46** with 17 of its 30 members higher, so the chip panic eased even as energy-sensitive and rate-sensitive Dow names lagged.

Where money moved was into defensive, recurring-revenue tech. **CrowdStrike** jumped **8.25%** to **$257.57**, and the cybersecurity complex rallied with it—**Palo Alto Networks** up **6.59%**, **Okta** up **6.26%**, **Cloudflare** up **6.02%**, **Datadog** up **5.00%**, and **Zscaler** up **4.93%**. The counterweight was crude: **WTI** pushed back above **$100** a barrel and **Brent** traded near **$115**, around 15-month highs, as the risk premium from the war with Iran held firm. For a market that spent the week arguing about whether AI spending will pay off, Friday was a reminder that the near-term tape is also hostage to the oil price.

### SpaceX Is Reportedly in Talks to Rent 80,000-Plus Cell Sites and Build a Fourth National Carrier

The day's biggest single move came from a report, not a release. **SpaceX** is in talks with tower operator **Crown Castle** to gain access to at least **80,000** U.S. cell sites as it works to stand up a fourth nationwide wireless carrier, Bloomberg reported. The market read it as a credible threat to the incumbents and a windfall for the landlords: **Crown Castle** surged more than **10%** and **American Tower** rose about **7%**, while **AT&T**, **Verizon**, and **T-Mobile** all fell on the competition fears.

The strategic logic is that SpaceX already has the hard part—spectrum and a satellite layer through Starlink—and renting existing ground infrastructure would let it add terrestrial capacity far faster than building towers from scratch. For the tower REITs, a new tenant with deep pockets is exactly the demand their business model is built to capture, which is why their shares moved opposite the carriers'. These remain talks rather than a signed deal, but the reaction shows how seriously the market now takes SpaceX as a would-be disruptor of U.S. telecom rather than merely a connectivity supplier to it.

### Earnings Keep Showing the AI Buildout Pulling Through to Storage, Energy, and Services

Away from the megacaps, Friday's prints reinforced a quieter pattern: the companies that equip the AI boom are booking the demand. **Western Digital** reported adjusted EPS of **$4.64**, beating the **$4.10** estimate and guiding above consensus, with management saying it has "good visibility" with its top seven customers out to **2030 and 2031**—a striking statement of how far ahead hyperscalers are now committing to storage, per Investrade's earnings roundup. The stock slipped about **2%** anyway, caught in the week's broader derisking.

The read-through extended to the physical plant around the data center. **SLB** beat with adjusted EPS of **$0.64** and disclosed that its **Data Center Solutions** revenue rose **76%** year over year to **$230 million**, with management flagging a strong 2027 outlook—an oilfield-services name increasingly selling into AI infrastructure. **Bloom Energy** beat on revenue (**$1.24 billion** versus **$1.07 billion** expected) at a **34.5%** gross margin and raised guidance, yet fell about **11%** as investors soured on power-and-AI-infrastructure plays after the week's spending scare. The divergence between solid numbers and falling stocks is the mood of the moment: the demand is real, but the market is done paying up for it on faith.

## GNSS News

### PlanetiQ Launches GNOMES-10, Turning GNSS Signals Into Weather and Space-Weather Data

The freshest positioning story pointed the technology outward, using satellite signals as a measuring instrument rather than a navigation aid. Colorado-based **PlanetiQ** launched its **GNOMES-10** radio-occultation satellite on **October 1** aboard SpaceX's **Transporter-18** rideshare from Vandenberg Space Force Base, with partner ground stations making first contact and commissioning beginning immediately, per GPS World (**October 15**). It is the latest addition to the company's commercial **GNSS radio occultation (GNSS-RO)** constellation, which profiles the atmosphere by watching how GNSS signals bend as they graze the Earth's limb.

The capability rests on the payload. GNOMES-10 carries PlanetiQ's fourth-generation **Pyxis** receiver, built and tested in Golden, Colorado, pairing a high-accuracy oscillator with a custom antenna to track **GPS, BeiDou, GLONASS, and Galileo** simultaneously across a **±75-degree** limb-sounding field of view—yielding more observations per hour and a signal-to-noise ratio high enough to resolve features in the lower troposphere and into the ionosphere. PlanetiQ says the goal is to "expand the availability, quality and diversity of commercial GNSS-RO observations" for weather forecasting and space weather, with a near-identical **GNOMES-11** slated to launch later in the month. It is a reminder that the same signals under attack over the Baltic are, elsewhere, quietly feeding the models that forecast the weather.

### Army, Navy, and Space Force Take Their 'Hammer of the Gods' Counter-PNT System to Sea

The complementary story came from the other end of the signal fight—contesting the electromagnetic environment rather than surviving it. The **U.S. Army**, **Navy**, and **Space Force** tested a maritime version of a system called **Hammer of the Gods**, a portable, low-signature command-and-control and electronic-warfare transmitter, taking it to sea for the first time during **Project Convergence Capstone 6 (PC-C6)** on the West Coast, per Inside GNSS (**October 13**). Land-based versions have been used in exercises for about two years; the maritime integration was supported out of **Naval Base San Diego**, with mesh networking used to extend the system's reach across platforms.

The point of the test was joint interoperability in a jammed and spoofed battlespace. The exercise focused on countering **PNT disruption**—the degradation of navigation, communications, precision-guided weapons, and uncrewed systems that an adversary's jamming can cause—and fed the Army's **Next Generation Command and Control (NGC2)** effort. "We wanted to validate joint operation across services in contested electromagnetic environments," said **Lt. Col. Darrin Hall** of U.S. Space Forces Pacific, noting that PNT disruption directly affects the precision of long-range artillery, guided missiles, and cruise munitions. Where most of the month's GNSS coverage has been about defending a receiver, this is the mirror image: a reminder that the same services buying resilient navigation are also rehearsing how to deny it.
