Tuesday's news kept circling one problem: the AI buildout is bumping into the physical world—power, cooling, real estate—and the answers are getting creative. SpaceX wants to put the data center in orbit; Emerald AI wants to make the ones on the ground flex with the grid; and Apple wants to shrink enough of the work onto a desktop that the cloud isn't always in the loop. South Korea, meanwhile, is turning its chip boom into the biggest national budget in its history. In positioning, the throughline was resilience for the moment GNSS stops working: a national bet on quantum navigation, and a demonstration that a drone can find its way home with nothing but a camera and a map.

## Tech News

### SpaceX Targets Late-2027 Launch for Its Nvidia-Powered Orbital Data Center

**SpaceX** plans to launch its first artificial-intelligence satellites—built around **Nvidia** chips—in the **fourth quarter of 2027**, with the orbital computing network reaching what **Elon Musk** called "significant scale" in **2028**, per Bloomberg. The two companies have designed a space-optimized version of Nvidia's **Vera Rubin NVL72** system, branded **Starmind**, with each satellite carrying Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs; Nvidia says its Space-1 module delivers up to **25 times** the AI performance of an H100. Musk described the satellite as "significantly simpler, lower cost, denser and lighter than a traditional rack."

The pitch is that orbit solves the two constraints strangling terrestrial data centers—power and cooling. In sunlight above the atmosphere, solar energy is abundant and radiative cooling is free, so the biggest operating costs of a ground facility mostly disappear. SpaceX envisions a constellation that could eventually grow to as many as **one million** satellites, stitched together with high-speed laser links into a distributed supercomputer. The near-term reality is far smaller and unproven—launch economics, radiation hardening, and servicing are all open questions—but the ambition reframes the same debate running through the rest of the day's news: when you can't build power fast enough on Earth, you look for somewhere the physics is friendlier.

### Apple's New Mac mini Bets on On-Device AI With the M6's Dual Neural Engine

**Apple** unveiled a new **Mac mini** on Tuesday powered by its all-new **M6** and **M5 Pro** chips, pitching the compact desktop as a platform for running AI models locally, per Apple and CNBC. The M6 version delivers up to **4x** faster AI performance, up to **40%** faster CPU, and roughly **2x** faster graphics than the prior generation, and it debuts the first-ever **dual Neural Engine**—two 16-core engines where earlier chips had one. Apple refreshed the **Mac Studio** alongside it with the same local-AI framing.

The strategy is the mirror image of SpaceX's. Where the orbital pitch is about massing compute somewhere cheaper, Apple's is about pushing enough of it onto the device that the user isn't always renting a data center at all. A cheaper, small-footprint machine that can run capable models on-device fits Apple's broader Apple Intelligence push toward assistants that act locally rather than round-tripping to the cloud—better for latency and privacy, and a hedge against the same compute-cost curve everyone else is financing. It also quietly turns on-device AI horsepower into a hardware-tier selling point rather than a universal software feature.

### Emerald AI Raises $150 Million to Turn Data Centers Into Flexible Grid Assets

**Emerald AI** raised **$150 million** in a Series A at a **$1.05 billion** valuation to scale software that lets AI data centers dynamically dial their power consumption up and down in response to grid conditions, per Business Wire. The oversubscribed round was co-led by **Energize Capital** and **DCVC**, bringing the company's total funding past **$220 million**. Emerald says its approach can unlock more than **100 gigawatts** of capacity on the existing U.S. grid—capacity that today sits stranded because utilities can't guarantee power for inflexible, always-on AI loads.

The idea attacks the AI buildout's hardest bottleneck from the opposite direction of a new power plant: instead of adding supply, make demand negotiable. Emerald's software is already deployed commercially at full data-center scale, and the company has partnered with **Silicon Valley Power** on a first-of-its-kind Flexible Load Interconnection Program that grants data centers faster grid access in exchange for dispatchable flexibility. It's also working with **Digital Realty** and **Nvidia** in Manassas, Virginia, on a nearly **100-megawatt** power-flexible AI facility. If moving compute to orbit is the moonshot answer to the power crunch, teaching ground-based centers to breathe with the grid is the pragmatic one—and investors just priced it at a billion dollars.

## GNSS News

### Canada Stands Up a $20.3 Million Quantum Defence Hub for GNSS-Independent Navigation

The most consequential positioning story of the day is a national bet on navigating without satellites. **Canada** launched its first **Quantum Defence Innovation Secure Hub (DISH)** in Calgary, a **University of Calgary**-led consortium that will receive **$20.3 million** over two years under the country's **BOREALIS** defense-innovation framework, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The hub organizes work around quantum sensing, quantum communications, quantum algorithms, and quantum hardware assurance, with **GNSS-independent navigation** and **spoofing detection** named among its top priorities.

The technology underneath is what makes it notable: quantum sensing, and specifically **atom interferometry**, can measure a platform's motion precisely enough to navigate for long stretches with no satellite signal at all—an inertial system that doesn't drift the way conventional ones do. That directly answers the threat that has dominated GNSS news all year, where jamming and spoofing have made unaided satellite navigation unreliable in contested airspace and waters. Rather than harden the signal, Canada is funding a class of sensor that doesn't need the signal in the first place—positioning quantum PNT as the long-horizon complement to the RF-based resilience tools already reaching the field.

### Leonardo and Telespazio Prove Drones Can Navigate GNSS-Denied by Camera Alone

On the nearer-term side, **Leonardo** and **Telespazio** completed a "Backup Solution for GNSS-Denied Navigation" project with the startup **Raysilience**, winner of the 2024 **T-TeC** technology contest, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The team—drawn from Rome's **La Sapienza** and **LUISS Guido Carli** universities—built a **Visual Positioning System** that lets a drone fix its location by matching what its camera sees against satellite imagery and interpolating between reference points, delivering reliable positioning with no GNSS input.

The appeal is that it's a plug-and-play backup rather than a bespoke platform. All planned deliverables were met, validating an approach designed to integrate across different aircraft and vehicles and switch in when the satellite link drops. Where Canada's quantum push is a multi-year science bet, visual positioning is buildable with cameras and compute that exist today—and it pairs naturally with inertial sensing to keep a UAV flying a mission through jamming or spoofing. Together the two stories sketch the emerging playbook for GNSS-denied navigation: quantum sensors for the resilience of the next decade, computer vision for the resilience you can ship now.
