Tuesday was about proof and money on the AI trade—and about a sky you can't fully trust. The infrastructure names that rent out AI compute delivered backlogs big enough to rescue a down day, venture firms opened new multibillion-dollar war chests to fund the next wave, and Meta's legal reckoning over kids kept advancing. In positioning, the through-line was interference: regulators treating GPS jamming as a safety crisis, and a defense supplier shipping a navigator built to shrug it off.

## Tech News

### CoreWeave and Supermicro Deliver the AI-Buildout Receipts, With $104 Billion and $60 Billion Backlogs

The clearest signal that AI demand is still outrunning supply came after Tuesday's close, when two of the buildout's key infrastructure suppliers reported and both stocks jumped in extended trading, per Yahoo Finance. **CoreWeave**, which rents GPU capacity to AI developers, posted second-quarter revenue of **$2.575 billion**—up **112%** year over year and past the roughly **$2.56 billion** consensus—and said its revenue backlog reached about **$104 billion** as of June 30, up from **$99.4 billion** a quarter earlier, per CNBC and Blockspace. The company added that the figure excludes more than **$25 billion** in net-new customer commitments signed in early July; the stock climbed about **10%**.

The profit picture is messier, and worth naming. CoreWeave's net loss widened **116%** to **$626 million** as interest costs on its debt-heavy expansion bit, though its **$1.14** per-share loss still beat expectations. **Super Micro Computer**, which builds the AI servers that house those chips, told a cleaner story: fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of **$11.1 billion** and adjusted EPS of **$1.70** (ahead of the **$1.59** expected), and—more importantly—first-quarter guidance of **$14.5–$15.5 billion**, towering over the roughly **$11.9 billion** analysts had penciled in, per Yahoo Finance. CEO **Charles Liang** said the company booked more than **$60 billion** in new orders and a record backlog entering fiscal 2027; shares rose more than **6%**. Both prints land the same punch: the spending everyone keeps fretting about is showing up as signed, backlogged demand.

### The Venture Money Piles Back In: Accel Raises $3.5 Billion, Sequoia Leans Back Into AI

If the earnings were the demand signal, the fundraising was the supply of capital chasing it. **Accel**—an early backer of **Anthropic**, **Cursor**, and **Perplexity**—has raised **$3.5 billion** across four new early-stage funds, per Bloomberg: a **$1.35 billion** global expansion fund for larger rounds and fast follow-ons, **$800 million** each for the U.S. and for Europe/Israel, and a **$550 million** India fund that was oversubscribed and closed within weeks, per TechCrunch. The firm framed AI as a horizontal layer now running under consumer internet, fintech, and manufacturing rather than a standalone category.

The move rhymes with the broader mood. Bloomberg separately reported that **Sequoia** is raising its bets on AI startups, tossing aside earlier caution to press harder into the race. Taken together, the two stories say the venture world has decided the AI cycle is worth funding aggressively again—right as the infrastructure players report the backlogs to match.

### Meta Loses Its Bid to Pause a Landmark Child-Safety Trial

**Meta** failed to persuade a judge to pause a landmark trial brought by a coalition of state attorneys general accusing the company of harming children's mental health, per Bloomberg—clearing the case to proceed after an eleventh-hour attempt to delay it. The setback compounds a rough stretch: days earlier, a **New Mexico** judge ordered Meta to pay **$567 million** to fund youth mental-health treatment and prevention, finding the company deliberately used endless scrolling, autoplay, and notifications to keep teens engaged, per NBC News and NPR.

That penalty stacks on top of the **$375 million** in civil penalties a jury levied in March, when it found Meta knowingly harmed children's mental health. The through-line is that the legal cost of Meta's engagement engine is no longer hypothetical—it's now measured in nine-figure judgments, with more trial exposure still ahead.

## GNSS News

### Aviation Regulators Escalate as GPS Jamming and Spoofing Hit "Crisis" Levels

The civil-aviation world is now treating satellite-navigation interference as a safety emergency rather than a nuisance. The **European Union Aviation Safety Agency** (EASA) has issued a revised Safety Information Bulletin warning that GNSS jamming and spoofing events are growing in both severity and sophistication, while the U.S. **FAA** rolled out Version 1.1 of its GPS/GNSS Interference Resource Guide covering interference trends, effects on aircraft systems, and recommended pilot procedures, per Runway Girl Network and AVweb. The updates land against a live backdrop: **safeairspace.net** logged fresh risk summaries this week, and **Lebanon** issued a NOTAM warning of jamming and spoofing in the Beirut FIR and advising crews to stick to conventional ATS routes.

The numbers explain the urgency. Industry data cited across the coverage tallied more than **430,000** GNSS jamming and spoofing incidents in 2024, affecting an estimated **700 to 1,350 flights per day**, with **IATA** flagging a **67%** rise in incidents in 2025 and a **193%** jump versus 2023, concentrated over Eastern Europe and the Middle East. It's the aviation face of the same resilience problem that has run through PNT all year—only here the stakes are aircraft flying published approaches on a signal that can be quietly bent out from under them, and the regulators' answer, for now, is guidance, training, and fallback procedures rather than a technical fix.

### Honeywell's Kestrel Ships a Drone Navigator Built for a Jammed Sky

The hardware answer to that same threat is getting smaller and cheaper. **Honeywell Aerospace's** **Kestrel** navigation system pairs the company's **HG3900** MEMS inertial measurement unit with both an **M-code** military-GPS receiver and a multi-GNSS receiver, so an uncrewed aircraft can lean on jam- and spoof-resistant M-code when the sky is contested and fall back to inertial dead-reckoning when there's no usable signal at all, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. Honeywell says the unit is about **40% smaller and lighter** than comparable products while delivering up to an **80%** improvement in navigation accuracy and cutting cost by as much as **50%**.

The target set is telling: Group 2 and 3 collaborative combat aircraft and loitering munitions—exactly the low-cost, GPS-reliant platforms most exposed to the jamming that has aviation regulators alarmed. Offered in international, non-ITAR configurations, Kestrel is a bet that resilient navigation has to become small, affordable, and embeddable to matter at the scale drones are now flown. Where the regulators are writing procedures for a degraded signal, Honeywell is trying to make the airframe stop caring whether the signal is there.
