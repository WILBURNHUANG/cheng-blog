Tuesday's news split along a clean line: who sells the AI buildout and who sells to consumers. The market's attention was on memory, where a wave of selling hit the chipmakers over fears that a Chinese rival is catching up faster than expected—pulling Asian indices down with it even as Wall Street's banks reported a trading bonanza. Apple, meanwhile, spent the day pointing the other direction entirely, unveiling a smart-home lineup aimed at the living room rather than the data center. And the one number that could swing the chip mood either way—ASML's quarterly order book—was still a day away. In positioning, the steady themes held: keep navigating when the satellite signal is gone, and rebuild the ground truth that everything else is measured against.

## Tech News

### Memory Chips Lead a Selloff on Fears China's CXMT Is Closing the DRAM Gap

The day's defining market move came from the memory makers. **Micron** fell about **5.19%**, **Western Digital** dropped **7.71%** in premarket trading, and **Seagate** slid **7.28%**, while in Asia **Samsung** lost **7.39%** and **SK Hynix** fell **4.29%**, dragging South Korea's KOSPI lower and helping push China's blue-chip index to a **14-month low**, according to market coverage from TheStreet. The catalyst was structural rather than cyclical: China's **ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT)** reportedly plans to roll out a new **4F²** DRAM cell structure by year-end, a density advance that stoked fears US and Korean dominance in memory is narrowing.

The broader tape stayed flat only because the damage was concentrated—the **S&P 500** closed at **7,805.45** (down **0.02%**), the **Nasdaq** at **27,204.38** (down **0.15%**), and the **Dow** at **51,670.10** (down **0.35%**), with technology among the weakest sectors. Western Digital took an extra hit after **UBS** cut its price target to **$470** from **$525**. The mood was captured by **Broadcom** CEO **Hock Tan**, who said investors "set very extreme expectations, only to find out that it's almost like they are in a casino gambling." After a year in which every chip name rode the same AI wave up, the memory selloff is the market starting to sort winners from the merely expensive.

### Apple Pushes Into the Smart Home at Its "Welcome Home" Event

Against the chip gloom, Apple made its pitch for the living room. The company held its **"Welcome Home"** event on **October 13** in Tribeca, a showcase Apple confirmed would center on the smart home, with a refreshed **HomePod mini** and next-generation **Apple TV 4K** among the lineup. The centerpiece, widely reported by Bloomberg's **Mark Gurman** ahead of the event, is a new smart display—referred to in reports as **"HomePad"**—built around Apple's new **Siri AI** assistant, with a roughly six-inch screen, a camera for household recognition, and a dedicated home operating system.

The significance is strategic: Apple is making its bet on ambient, on-device AI as a consumer product while rivals pour capital into training infrastructure. By routing the smart home through a redesigned Siri rather than the cloud, Apple is leaning on the privacy-and-device story it has pushed all year. It also marks the company's most serious challenge yet to Amazon's Echo and Google's Nest in a category it has largely ceded. The event is separate from Apple's previously announced **October 22** Mac and iPad showcase—two hardware pushes inside two weeks, aimed at the holiday quarter.

### ASML's Q3 Report Lands Wednesday as the Order-Book Test for the AI Rally

The chip debate gets its most important data point on Wednesday. **ASML**, the Dutch maker of the EUV lithography machines that every advanced fab depends on, reports third-quarter results before the European market opens, with the company guiding to net sales of **€11 billion to €12 billion** and a gross margin of **55% to 57%**, against a full-year 2026 outlook of **€43 billion to €45 billion** raised in July, per preview coverage from Investing.com and others. Consensus sits near **€11.7 billion** in revenue.

The number that matters most is orders. As a monopoly supplier at the front of the semiconductor supply chain, ASML's bookings are the cleanest forward read on whether the fabs building AI silicon are still committing capital—or pausing. The report lands at a nervous moment: the same memory-chip selloff rattling the sector this week makes the order book either the reassurance the rally needs or the confirmation that caution is setting in. After a month in which chip executives themselves warned that capital, not supply, is now the binding constraint, ASML's backlog is where that thesis gets tested with real numbers.

## GNSS News

### Elsight Launches Cellular-Based Positioning for GPS-Denied Uncrewed Systems at AUSA 2026

The freshest positioning news was a way to keep drones oriented when the satellite signal is gone. **Elsight** (ASX: ELS) launched a new **Positioning** capability built into its **Halo** connectivity platform at **AUSA 2026** on **October 12**, determining a platform's absolute location from cellular-network measurements rather than GPS, per a company release carried by DRONELIFE and Military Embedded Systems. A multi-source fusion engine blends cellular **Timing Advance**, nearby base-station signals, verified GPS when reliable, and motion and velocity data, defaulting to GPS but switching to cellular when the signal is jammed or absent.

The move matters because it treats resilience as a software unlock, not a hardware swap—existing Halo customers get the capability with no added equipment. It also folds in active spoofing detection, comparing the GPS fix against the cellular-derived position and flagging when the two diverge. The target is uncrewed air, land, and maritime platforms operating in contested, dark, or at-sea environments where GPS is denied. "GPS has become a critical vulnerability in contested environments," said CEO **Yoav Amitai**, framing the product's edge simply: "Every alternative tells you how far you've moved. We tell you where you are." With Halo reporting more than **500,000** operational hours across **100-plus** customers, Elsight is pushing an absolute-positioning backstop into a fleet already in the field.

### US National Geodetic Survey Opens Public Testing of Its Modernized Reference System

The complementary story was about rebuilding the ground truth under all of it. The **National Geodetic Survey (NGS)** released **Core Beta Products and Tools** for public testing ahead of formally adopting the modernized **National Spatial Reference System (NSRS)**, with its first public webinar held **October 1** and the rollout detailed by GPS World on **October 7**. After at least six months of testing, the beta tools can move to production and NGS will publish the new NSRS—the biggest overhaul in decades of how US positions are defined.

The significance is foundational. A new **Data Delivery System** becomes the main gateway for geodetic data, replacing the old CORS station pages and ASCII datasheets with interactive **Geodetic Station** and **Geodetic Mark** pages that let users pick among modernized reference frames—**ITRF2020, NATRF2022, CATRF2022, MATRF2022,** and **PATRF2022**. For surveyors, engineers, and the growing base of RTK and PPP users who depend on precise, consistent coordinates, the change means datum shifts and time-dependent positions they will have to plan for. It is the civil-infrastructure counterpart to the week's defense-side resilience work: before you can navigate precisely, everyone has to agree on where "here" actually is.
