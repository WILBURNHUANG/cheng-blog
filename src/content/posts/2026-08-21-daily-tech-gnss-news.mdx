Friday's news was less about new products than about new mechanics—how AI capital gets deployed, how autonomy gets permitted, and where the people who build it choose to live. Nvidia found a way to absorb a startup's models and engineers without an acquisition; Nevada handed Tesla room to run robotaxis at a scale no U.S. regulator has yet allowed; and a labor-market report confirmed that AI's center of gravity is drifting east. In positioning, the throughline was low Earth orbit graduating from concept to ecosystem: a commercial constellation lining up certified receivers, and a mass-market chipmaker sizing up Europe's first LEO-PNT demonstrators.

## Tech News

### Nvidia Pays Poolside $6 Billion to License Its Models—and Hire Its People—Without Buying the Company

**Nvidia** has struck an unusual arrangement with AI coding startup **Poolside** that bundles technology licensing, an investment, and mass hiring while stopping short of an outright acquisition, per Newcomer. The chipmaker will pay roughly **$6 billion** under a non-exclusive licensing agreement and invest another **$1 billion** in Poolside at a **$12 billion** pre-money valuation, while extending job offers to about **109** of the startup's employees. Poolside keeps operating as an independent company.

The structure is the story. Rather than buy a startup and swallow the regulatory and integration headaches that come with it, a cash-rich acquirer can license the models, hire the team that built them, and leave the corporate shell running—giving investors liquidity and fresh capital without a traditional sale. It echoes the "acqui-hire without the acquisition" playbook that Big Tech has leaned on to sidestep antitrust scrutiny, now scaled to eleven figures. For Nvidia, the deal pushes it past chips and infrastructure into the software and model layer, specifically AI coding, deepening its reach into how its own hardware gets used.

### Tesla Wins Nevada Approval for Up to 5,000 Las Vegas Robotaxis

**Tesla** received Nevada regulatory approval to deploy as many as **5,000** robotaxis in the Las Vegas area over the next year, per TechCrunch—a fleet cap five times larger than the **1,000-vehicle** authorizations granted to **Waymo** and **Uber**. The permits give Elon Musk's company far more room to scale its autonomous ride-hailing ambitions than it has had in any U.S. market to date.

The size of the grant is what makes Las Vegas a proving ground. Tesla has staked much of its future valuation on robotaxis but still faces open questions about safety performance, regulatory compliance, and whether its camera-and-software approach can operate at scale without human supervision. Waymo, by contrast, already runs thousands of vehicles nationally and has expanded city by city. Nevada's willingness to authorize a fleet this large in one metro tests whether Tesla can convert its software-centric strategy into real-world deployment—and turns the Strip into the most closely watched autonomy market in the country.

### New York Overtakes the Bay Area as the Top U.S. Tech-Talent Market

For the first time in the 13 years **CBRE** has tracked it, **New York** has passed the **San Francisco Bay Area** as the largest U.S. tech-talent market, with roughly **394,300** tech jobs to the Bay Area's **375,730**, per CNBC's reporting on the report. The shift was driven by finance-sector technology and AI hiring in New York colliding with job cuts on the West Coast.

AI is the engine reshaping the map. AI-related roles now make up nearly a third of U.S. tech job listings and grew about **45%** year over year across the U.S. and Canada, with both markets adding more than **20,000** AI-specific positions since mid-2025. As enterprise and financial-services demand for AI talent concentrates in New York, the ranking signals a real change in where startups and Big Tech will compete for scarce specialists—and a challenge to the assumption that frontier AI work has to happen in Silicon Valley.

## GNSS News

### Xona Signs Up the Receiver Ecosystem for Its Pulsar LEO-PNT Constellation

The most consequential positioning trend right now is low Earth orbit moving from demo to deployment, and **Xona Space Systems** is building the ecosystem to receive it. Its **Pulsar Verified** program—launched in July to certify that receivers, chipsets, and test equipment interoperate with Xona's LEO positioning, navigation, and timing signal—keeps adding heavyweight partners, with **Safran Electronics & Defense** the latest to join, per GPS World. That extends a roster that already includes **Trimble**, **Septentrio**, **STMicroelectronics**, **StarNav**, and **Keysight**.

The point of a verification program is to solve the chicken-and-egg problem every new signal faces: hardware makers won't build for a constellation that isn't up, and a constellation is worthless without hardware that can hear it. Xona plans a **258-satellite** LEO network broadcasting signals up to **100 times stronger** than GPS, with authenticated protection against spoofing and jamming and centimeter-level precision, and initial satellites are slated to begin launching in **late 2026**. Because Pulsar is designed to complement rather than replace the existing GPS ecosystem, lining up certified receivers now means real products can be in the field roughly when the birds are. It's a commercial bet that the resilience gap civil users have been sounding alarms over all year gets filled from orbit, not just from the ground.

### u-blox Starts Evaluating ESA's Celeste LEO-PNT Signals for the Mass Market

On the European side, the same LEO-PNT wave reached the mass-market chip layer. Following the March **28** launch of ESA's first **Celeste** in-orbit demonstration satellites (**IOD-1** and **IOD-2**), **u-blox** said it is evaluating how low-Earth-orbit signals can augment established GNSS constellations like **Galileo**, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The work runs under ESA's **NAVISP Element 2** program and centers on integrating LEO signal reception into u-blox's **X20** GNSS platform, including signal characterization and system-level design.

What makes this notable is who's doing the evaluating. u-blox builds positioning chips for high-volume consumer and industrial devices, so its interest signals that LEO-PNT is being taken seriously as a future building block for everyday hardware—not just defense and survey niches. Celeste's first phase is an **11-satellite** demonstration constellation testing signals across multiple frequency bands, and its appeal is structural: lower orbits deliver stronger signals and rapidly changing geometry that can speed convergence and improve resilience where traditional GNSS struggles. Paired with Xona's commercial push, it's a two-continent sign that the industry now treats LEO as a serious complement to the medium-orbit constellations that have defined satellite navigation for decades.
