Wednesday's news kept circling the same two questions the year has posed all along: who is financing the AI buildout, and who gets to set its rules. On the money side, a chip-leasing deal first disclosed as a straightforward loan has turned into a layered credit structure that now reaches from the biggest banks to private-credit giants—while lenders pile into riskier chip loans across Asia. On the rules side, Washington rebranded the technology and built a task force around it, and the sector's largest chipmaker tried to answer the safety question with a product and a historic buyback on the same day. In positioning, the steady lesson held once more—assume the satellite signal will be jammed or gone—expressed this time through a military anti-jam contract and a new American factory for navigation that works where GPS cannot reach.

## Tech News

### Update: The Broadcom-Anthropic Chip Loan Becomes a Record $60 Billion Syndicated Package

The financing deal that led last week's news has grown into something larger and more layered. **Bank of America, Citigroup, and Morgan Stanley** are syndicating a record **$60 billion** package to fund **Anthropic's** lease of advanced chips from **Broadcom** and **Google**, structured as **$42 billion** in senior secured debt—the portion Broadcom had agreed to backstop—plus **$18 billion** in junior financing, with **Blackstone** committing **$9 billion** to the junior tranche, per AdvisorHub. The original disclosure framed the arrangement as a vendor loan; the new detail is how broadly the risk is now being sold.

The significance is the shift from equity to credit as AI's funding engine. What began as a chipmaker lending a customer money to rent its own silicon has become a syndicated-debt deal drawing in the largest US banks and a marquee private-credit lender—moving AI-infrastructure exposure onto balance sheets well beyond the technology sector. It arrives alongside Bloomberg's reporting that banks are chasing increasingly risky chip loans across Asia's **$8.2 trillion** AI buildout, a sign the same credit appetite is spreading globally. The more the buildout leans on borrowed money, the more a stumble in AI demand becomes a problem for lenders, not just for the labs doing the spending.

### Trump Rebrands AI as "Super Intelligence" and Stands Up a Federal Task Force

Washington moved to reframe the entire race. President **Trump** signed an executive order rebranding artificial intelligence as "**super intelligence**" (SI) and created a federal **Super Intelligence Force** to coordinate efforts to keep American leadership, comparing the technology's significance to the Industrial Revolution and the internet, per TechCrunch. The task force is chaired by National Intelligence Director **Jay Clayton**, with vice chairs drawn from the FTC, the War Department, and the Office of Personnel Management, and has **120 days** to produce a report on SI-enabled risks and opportunities.

The charter's framing is the tell. It pairs language about "responding to SI-enabled threats" with an explicit aim of "preventing overregulation and regulatory capture that would stifle innovation," and Clayton cast the primary danger as "not being first" relative to China—a posture that treats speed as the safety strategy. The order was accompanied by a non-binding safety pledge signed by tech executives rather than binding rules. For an industry spending the month on compute financing and safety crises, the message from Washington is that the federal government intends to accelerate, not brake.

### Nvidia Pairs a Platform to Rein In Rogue AI Agents With a $150 Billion Buyback

The sector's most valuable chipmaker tried to answer the safety question and reward shareholders in one move. **Nvidia** unveiled a security platform designed to keep AI agents from "going rogue" and, on the same day, authorized a **$150 billion** stock repurchase—the largest buyback in US corporate history—per inkl, with CEO **Jensen Huang** tying the security effort to partners including **Anthropic** and **Hugging Face**. Shares rose on the combination of the safety product and the capital return.

The pairing captures the moment Nvidia is in. As AI agents gain the ability to browse, call tools, and touch real systems, the gap between what a model is allowed to do and what it actually does has become an operational problem customers need solved—so selling the guardrails is itself a growth market. Doing it alongside a record buyback signals a company so flush with AI-driven cash that it can fund the largest repurchase on record while still investing in the next layer of the stack. It is the confident counterpoint to the week's financing anxieties: the one firm at the center of the boom returning cash rather than borrowing it.

## GNSS News

### BAE Systems Wins a Contract to Put Anti-Jam M-Code GPS on Navy Carrier Landings

The freshest positioning news hardened a safety-critical military system against jamming. **BAE Systems** will supply its **DIGAR-500M** Digital GPS Anti-Jam Receiver to upgrade the Navy's **Joint Precision Approach and Landing System (JPALS)** with military **M-code** capability, under a **$16 million** award from **Collins Aerospace**, with work based in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, per GPS World. JPALS guides aircraft to precise carrier landings in all weather, and the upgrade enables dual **SAASM** and M-code operation with up to **24 steered beams** of digital beamforming for anti-jam and anti-spoof protection.

The contract matters because it moves resilient GPS into one of the least forgiving use cases there is. A carrier landing leaves no room for a degraded fix, and layering encrypted M-code signals onto a digitally beam-steered receiver attacks jamming and spoofing at the same time—nulling out interference while authenticating the military signal. Against a year of guidance telling operators to assume the satellite signal can be taken away, this is the hardware answer for the missions that cannot simply fall back to dead reckoning: build the anti-jam protection directly into the receiver that flies the approach.

### Advanced Navigation Breaks Ground on a US Factory for GPS-Denied Navigation

The complementary story was about building the supply chain for navigation that works without satellites. **Advanced Navigation America** is establishing a **Positioning, Navigation and Timing Center of Excellence** in **Huntsville, Alabama**, to develop and manufacture systems for **GPS-denied** environments, backed by the company's **$110 million** Series C round, per Inside GNSS. The facility will produce strategic-grade **fiber optic gyroscopes**, **laser velocity sensors**, and counter-electromagnetic-warfare technology—precision instruments the company likens in complexity to semiconductor fabrication—for a base of more than **100,000** systems deployed worldwide.

The move matters because assured PNT is becoming, in the words of Advanced Navigation America president **Mike Cohen**, "the backbone of modern autonomy," and that now requires domestic manufacturing. The Huntsville site targets two very different GPS-denied frontiers at once: underground mines, where haul trucks and drill rigs lose satellite signal entirely, and classified defense programs for customers including Anduril, Kongsberg, BAE Systems, and Lockheed Martin. Its proximity to Redstone Arsenal and U.S. Space Command underscores the strategic logic—onshoring the inertial sensors that let vehicles navigate when the satellites are jammed, spoofed, or simply out of reach.
