Thursday's news turned Nvidia's earnings relief into action on two fronts—an acquisition that reaches up the stack toward developers, and a market that finally let its enthusiasm spread past the chipmaker to the software names built on top of it. Salesforce's blowout, powered by an Anthropic tie-up, led a software rebound even as the rally stayed narrow. And a fresh round for driverless freight showed the AI money still flowing into moving atoms, not just bits. In positioning, the story of the day was low-Earth-orbit navigation crossing from demo to product: a U.S. startup rallying the receiver industry behind its commercial signal, and Europe advancing a state-backed demonstrator toward the same goal.

## Tech News

### Nvidia Moves to Buy Hugging Face for ~$12.9 Billion, Reaching for the Open-Source AI Layer

One day after guidance that sent its shares up roughly **9%**, **Nvidia** agreed to acquire **Hugging Face**—the GitHub-like hub where developers share and download open-source AI models—in a deal valuing the startup at about **$12.9 billion**, per The Information, CNBC, and Fortune. Founded in **2016**, Hugging Face has become the default public square for open models, and the acquisition would push Nvidia beyond silicon into the software and distribution layer that sits directly above it.

The logic is defensive and expansionary at once. Owning the place where millions of developers build gives Nvidia a hedge against customers like OpenAI and Google designing their own accelerators, and a foothold back in the cloud and tooling business, per Bloomberg. Reports cautioned the talks had not produced a signed agreement and could still fall apart, but a bid of this size for a model repository—rather than a chip or data-center asset—signals where Nvidia thinks the next competitive moat lies: not just in making the hardware, but in owning the ecosystem that decides what runs on it.

### Salesforce Rockets ~22% on Earnings Beat and a Deepened Anthropic Partnership

**Salesforce** posted fiscal Q2 revenue of **$11.35 billion**, up **11%** year over year, and raised its full-year forecast to **$46.1–$46.4 billion**, but the stock's roughly **22%** surge—its second-best day ever, trailing only August 2020—was as much about AI positioning as the print, per CNBC and The Motley Fool. Alongside the results, the company unveiled an expanded partnership with **Anthropic** and a new **"Claudeforce"** integration that gives Claude users access to Salesforce data and **37** pre-built sales skills, with an open beta expected in September.

The move reframes the "will AI eat SaaS?" anxiety that had weighed on Salesforce all year. Rather than treat model makers as a threat, Salesforce is wiring **Claude** into its own workflows and booking a **$2.6 billion** gain on its stake in the AI startup for good measure, per Seeking Alpha. The market read it as proof that incumbent software can turn AI into a demand tailwind instead of a disruption, and the enthusiasm spilled over: ServiceNow, Adobe, and a basket of software peers rallied on the idea that the sector's slump may be ending.

### Gatik Raises $200 Million to Scale Driverless Freight

Autonomous-trucking company **Gatik** raised **$200 million** as it pushes driverless middle-mile freight from pilots toward commercial scale, per Tech Startups. The company specializes in short-haul, fixed-route deliveries between distribution points—a narrower, more repeatable problem than open-highway autonomy—which has let it move toward removing safety drivers on defined lanes.

The round is a reminder that AI capital is still funding the physical economy, not only chatbots and data centers. Where much of the week's money chased compute and models, Gatik's raise backs the less glamorous work of applying autonomy to logistics, where the payoff is measured in delivery costs and labor rather than tokens. It lands as investors keep writing large early checks for AI-adjacent businesses that promise concrete unit economics.

## GNSS News

### Xona's Pulsar Verified Rallies the Receiver Industry Behind Commercial LEO-PNT

The clearest sign that low-Earth-orbit navigation is going commercial is an ecosystem forming around it. **Xona Space Systems** launched its **Pulsar Verified** certification program, giving partners a technical path to validate that receivers, chipsets, and simulators work with its **Pulsar** LEO positioning, navigation, and timing signal, per Inside GNSS and SpaceNews. The inaugural cohort spans **Trimble**, **Septentrio**, **STMicroelectronics**, **Safran**, **StarNav**, and **Keysight**, and Safran's **Skydel** and Keysight's **PNT X** GNSS simulators have already been verified so developers can test against the signal before hardware ships.

The timing matters because the plumbing is now cleared. The program follows the **FCC's** authorization for Xona to deploy its full commercial **Pulsar** LEO constellation, with initial satellites slated to begin service as the network comes online, per SatNews. Pitched as delivering signals far stronger than legacy GPS and harder to jam or spoof from orbit, Pulsar aims to layer resilient, high-accuracy PNT beneath everything from drones and robots to phones and IoT devices—and a verification program is how Xona turns a constellation into something integrators can actually design around today.

### Europe's ESA Advances Celeste as a Public-Sector LEO-PNT Answer

Running in parallel is a state-backed bet on the same idea. The **European Space Agency's** **Celeste** in-orbit demonstrator—led by **GMV** with **Alén Space**—has broadcast the first dual-frequency L- and S-band navigation message ever sent from a European satellite in low Earth orbit, per ESA and GPS World. GMV serves as prime contractor for six demonstrator satellites spanning the space, ground, and user segments, and the program is now working through its in-orbit demonstration phase.

The strategic aim is sovereignty as much as performance. Celeste is meant to pave the way for a European multi-layer PNT architecture and inform the EU's decision on an operational **LEO** navigation layer that would complement **Galileo** and **EGNOS**, prompting chipmakers like **u-blox** to publicly assess how LEO signals could slot into mass-market positioning. Together with Xona's commercial push, the two efforts sketch the shape of the coming decade: a private constellation racing to ship a resilient signal now, and a public program building the European alternative—both convinced that the future of trustworthy positioning sits far below the altitude where GPS and Galileo fly.
