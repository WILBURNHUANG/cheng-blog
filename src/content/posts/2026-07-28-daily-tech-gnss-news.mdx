The market's AI anxiety found a new and sharper trigger today, and it came from an unexpected direction: not another capex warning, but a manufacturing milestone in Shanghai. A report that China has cracked a piece of chipmaking equipment the West assumed was safely locked up sent semiconductor stocks into a global rout, deepening a selloff that has now tipped the Nasdaq 100 toward correction. Yet in the same session Apple crossed a threshold no company but Nvidia had ever reached, a reminder that "the AI trade" is really several trades pointing different ways. And from inside the labs themselves came an unusual plea for restraint. In positioning, a sober annual report made official what jamming maps have implied for two years.

## Tech News

### China's Lithography Breakthrough Triggers a Global Chip Rout

A single report reset the semiconductor market's mood on Tuesday: Shanghai-based, state-backed **Shanghai Yuliangsheng** has reportedly begun mass-producing homegrown **deep-ultraviolet (DUV) lithography** machines—a category **ASML** has effectively monopolized—with the first deliveries to leading Chinese chipmakers expected later this year, per Bloomberg and Yahoo Finance. The prospect of China closing the gap on its most stubborn chokepoint hit equipment makers hardest: ASML fell more than **7%**, dragging Applied Materials, Lam Research, and KLA down with it, while Nvidia dropped roughly **5%**, AMD more than **8%**, and Micron close to **6%**.

The damage was worse in Asia. South Korea's **Kospi** plunged nearly **11%**—its steepest one-day drop in months—as memory giants **Samsung** and **SK Hynix** suffered their worst single-session losses in almost two decades, with Samsung closing down about **13%** and SK Hynix around **14.5%**, per Bloomberg. The selloff pushed the tech-heavy **Nasdaq 100** toward a correction, more than 10% off its recent record. The read-through is what unsettled investors: if China can build its own advanced fabrication tools, the export controls meant to preserve a Western lead lose some of their bite, and the pricing power baked into today's chip valuations looks less secure. After weeks of arguing about whether AI spending would pay off, the market got a new worry—whether the moat around the hardware is as wide as everyone assumed.

### Apple Briefly Touches $5 Trillion, the Second Company Ever

Even in a red session, **Apple** made history, briefly crossing a **$5 trillion** market capitalization on Tuesday to become just the second publicly traded company ever to reach the mark, per Bloomberg and CNBC. The stock touched an intraday high of **$342.89**, up about 1.8%, before easing back; Apple had already passed Nvidia on Monday to reclaim the title of world's most valuable company. **Nvidia** was the first to $5 trillion, hitting it in October 2025—and Apple got here less than a year after clearing $4 trillion, riding a roughly **24%** year-to-date gain and near-60% climb over twelve months on durable iPhone demand.

The timing carries weight beyond the number. Apple reaches the milestone days ahead of its quarterly report on Thursday—the last earnings call for CEO **Tim Cook**, who hands the reins to **John Ternus** on September 1. That Apple hit the peak on a day the chipmakers were being punished underscores a split inside the "AI trade": Wall Street is docking the companies building the picks and shovels while rewarding the one that has said the least about its AI spending and the most about selling devices people already want. Whether that calm survives contact with the earnings call is Thursday's question.

### More Than 1,100 AI Workers Ask Washington to Slow the Race

In a rare move from inside the industry, more than **1,100 employees** of leading AI companies—including **OpenAI**, **Anthropic**, **Google**, and **Meta**—signed an open letter urging the U.S. government to back an international effort to build the technical and governance tools needed to "deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development," per Bloomberg. The signatories are not fringe voices: they include Anthropic cofounders **Jack Clark** and **Jared Kaplan**, OpenAI chief scientist **Jakub Pachocki**, Meta chief scientist **Shengjia Zhao**, and Google DeepMind's safety and alignment lead **Anca Dragan**.

The letter's target is specific—"automated AI development," the point at which AI systems become capable of improving themselves, a loop the field calls **recursive self-improvement**—and it warns of a "real risk" that the technology advances faster than people can understand or control it. Coming the same week the market is punishing AI's costs, it adds a second kind of unease to the narrative: not just whether the spending pays off, but whether the people closest to the frontier think the pace itself is safe. That researchers racing to build the next model are simultaneously asking their own government to help slow the race is the sort of contradiction that tends to shape policy.

## GNSS News

### 'A Constant of Modern Conflict': SWF Report Formalizes the Interference Era

The **Secure World Foundation's** 2026 **Global Counterspace Capabilities** report landed this week with a blunt framing that the navigation community has been circling for two years: GNSS interference has shifted from an episodic threat to a persistent feature of conflict zones on multiple continents, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The report assesses counterspace programs across 13 countries in five categories—co-orbital, direct-ascent, electronic warfare, directed energy, and cyber—and treats electronic warfare against positioning signals as a now-routine instrument of statecraft rather than an exceptional act.

The document also stitches together the regulatory backlash. It notes that in October 2025 the **ICAO** passed a resolution condemning GNSS interference from **Russia** and **North Korea** as violations of the 1944 Chicago Convention, and that the **ITU's** Radio Regulations Board urged Russia to "immediately cease" harmful interference affecting receivers in Estonia, Finland, Latvia, and Lithuania. Most striking is a technical finding the report highlights: GPS jamming over Ukraine has punched "a giant hole" in coverage for small **low-Earth-orbit satellites**, meaning the interference is now degrading space-segment PNT, not just ground users. The takeaway for anyone building receivers is the same one driving the whole resilience push—design for a signal that will sometimes be denied, because denial is no longer the exception.

### VIAVI Extends LEO-Based Timing Into Maritime Tracking

On the commercial side, **VIAVI Solutions** is bringing its LEO-based PNT service to a maritime application, integrating its **SecureTime altGNSS** capability into a vessel-tracking platform so ships can hold precise position and timing when conventional GPS is jammed or spoofed, per Inside GNSS. The approach leans on a software-defined receiver that draws timing from low-Earth-orbit signals and can maintain synchronization through outages—purpose-built for what the defense world calls Denied, Degraded, and Disrupted Space Operational Environments.

It is a small deployment with an outsized message: LEO-PNT is quietly moving off the demo bench and into the kind of unglamorous operational niches—tracking cargo, timing networks—where a new signal proves it can actually do a job. Layered PNT keeps advancing one integration at a time.
