The AI trade found its feet again. After the worst week for stocks since June, Monday brought a broad, forceful bounce—chips led, volatility cratered, and three-quarters of the S&P 500 closed higher. Yet the session carried a sharp asterisk in the form of Nvidia, the stock most responsible for the year's gains, which sold off on a governance scandal that had nothing to do with earnings and everything to do with espionage. Beneath the rebound, the plumbing told a more cautious story: oil tumbled on easing Iran tensions, a big bank cut its call on U.S. equities, and the fastest-money investors were said to be fleeing tech even as the indexes rallied. In positioning, the week opened with hardware built to keep working when the signals get noisy—and a reminder that even a Moon rocket can scramble GPS.

## Tech News

### AI Stocks Rebound Hard as Chips Lead and Volatility Collapses

Wall Street came back swinging to start the week. The **Nasdaq Composite** jumped **2.74%** to **25,253.14**, the **S&P 500** rose **2.35%** to **7,549.94**, and the **Dow Jones Industrial Average** added **2.15%**—**1,104 points**—to **52,389.75**, while the small-cap **Russell 2000** outran them all with a **3.17%** gain to **2,841.45**, per market data from Yahoo Finance. The tech-heavy **Nasdaq-100** climbed **3.40%**. The fear that had gripped the tape for a week drained out fast: the **Cboe Volatility Index** collapsed **21.9%** to **20.02**, unwinding most of the spike that had carried it to 28 during Thursday's rout.

Semiconductors did the heavy lifting. **Coherent** soared about **18%** on strong earnings tied to AI optical demand, **AMD** surged roughly **13%** as Wall Street revived the AI-chip trade following an Oppenheimer upgrade, and the rally spread to the AI-power complex, with **Bloom Energy** up **13.5%**, contract manufacturer **Flex** up **12.9%** on an earnings beat and raised guidance, and **Ferguson** up **12.5%**. Breadth confirmed the move—about **75%** of S&P 500 members rose and nearly every sector advanced—though analysts cautioned the recovery looked tentative rather than decisive.

### Nvidia Sinks as Its Board Opens a Probe Into Jensen Huang

The glaring exception to the rally was its usual leader. **Nvidia** fell about **5%** after the company disclosed that its board had opened an internal investigation into reports that CEO **Jensen Huang** had a close relationship with a person described as a suspected Chinese intelligence operative, per Yahoo Finance. The board said it has "no reason to believe" company secrets were compromised and reiterated its confidence in Huang's leadership, and analysts said there is no confirmed sign he will step down. President **Trump**, for his part, said Huang should stay on as CEO.

The timing was awkward on two fronts. The controversy threw a high-profile Nvidia event in Korea into disarray, according to Bloomberg, and it landed just as the broader chip trade was trying to heal—leaving the world's most valuable chipmaker as a conspicuous red mark on an otherwise green board. With Nvidia's year-to-date gains already whittled down by last week's selloff, a scandal with national-security overtones is exactly the kind of idiosyncratic risk the AI trade can least afford while sentiment remains fragile.

### Oil Slides on Iran Hopes as the Smart Money Heads for the Exits

The commodity and positioning signals cut against the equity euphoria. **WTI crude** fell about **4.1%** to **$87.07** a barrel and **Brent** slid roughly **3.6%** to **$94.75**—both well off the triple digits they touched during the recent Strait of Hormuz scare—after Trump played down the prospect of renewed strikes on Iran, feeding hopes of a U.S.-Iran accommodation. Havens still caught a bid despite the risk-on tone in stocks: **gold** rose **1.8%** to **$4,149** an ounce and **Bitcoin** edged up **1.0%** to about **$74,800**.

Under the surface, the people who trade for a living were selling. Bloomberg reported that **hedge funds dumped technology stocks at one of the fastest paces on record** during the unwind, and **Citigroup** strategists downgraded U.S. equities and the tech sector to neutral while upgrading European stocks—a vote of no-confidence in the very trade that powered Monday's bounce. The split between a surging index and a retreating fast-money crowd is the market's central unresolved tension heading deeper into November.

## GNSS News

### Trimble Debuts an All-in-One Machine-Control Unit With Anti-Jamming GNSS

The week's freshest positioning hardware folded a construction machine's entire electronics stack into a single box. On **November 9** at its **Trimble Dimensions User Conference**, Trimble introduced the **MP1087**, an all-in-one machine-positioning solution for its **Earthworks** grade-control platform that merges GNSS positioning and communications hardware—previously sold as separate components—into one compact, rugged unit, per The American Surveyor. The design is meant to cut installation time and simplify support, and it bundles Wi-Fi, cellular, UHF radio and network connectivity (including **Wi-Fi HaLow**) for moving project data on and off the machine.

The resilience story sits in the receiver. The MP1087 is built on Trimble's next-generation **Maxwell 8** GNSS technology and adds **anti-jamming and anti-spoofing** protection—features that have become table stakes as interference spreads from conflict zones into everyday worksites. Trimble says the unit delivers up to a **15%** uptime improvement in challenging environments such as tree cover and urban canyons, about **10x** faster display-to-unit communication and roughly **5x** more processing power than the hardware it replaces. "Downtime costs customers money" in a thin-margin industry, said **Elwyn McLachlan**, Trimble's VP of civil construction product and strategy. The MP1087 ships worldwide through Trimble's dealer network starting **late Q4 2026** and is included in Trimble Works and Works Plus subscriptions.

### A Study Finds the Artemis II Launch Tore Three 5,000-km Holes in the Ionosphere

A reminder arrived this week that GNSS can be disrupted from above as well as from the ground. A new study in **Geophysical Research Letters**, flagged by TechRadar and Hackaday, found that NASA's **Artemis II** launch—the first crewed flight toward lunar space since 1972—created three large disturbances in the **ionosphere**, each more than **5,000 km** (about 3,100 miles) across and all over North America. The disturbances came from both the initial liftoff and the upper-stage engines firing high in the atmosphere, and the researchers say the effect was stronger than any previous rocket launch, including SpaceX's—a phenomenon they term "anthropogenic space weather."

The navigation implications are the hook. Because the ionosphere bends and delays the radio signals that GNSS receivers rely on, such tears can cause "significant but temporary" shifts in positioning. The study did not directly measure the GPS error and the team plans follow-up work, but lead author **Charles Lin**, an earth scientist at National Cheng Kung University in Taiwan, put the stakes plainly to Space.com: "A positioning error of a couple of meters is enough to cause an autonomous car to change lanes." He also flagged possible effects on drone positioning—underscoring that as self-driving systems lean harder on precise GNSS, even a Moon shot becomes a source of navigation risk.
