Monday's news kept circling the same question from different angles: what do the dominant players do when everyone around them is building an escape hatch? Nvidia's answer was to fund and equip the very custom-silicon effort meant to lessen dependence on it, betting it can cede chips while keeping the data center. Apple's was a generational handoff, trading its longest-serving CEO for a hardware engineer just as the company scrambles on AI. And Anthropic's was to sue—and win—against a government that tried to freeze it out. In positioning, the throughline was the search for signals that keep working when GPS doesn't: a C-band alternative proven under jamming, and AI trained to spot a fake satellite signal before it steers anything.

## Tech News

### Nvidia Invests $3.5 Billion in MediaTek, Handing Rivals the Tools to Build Custom AI Chips

**Nvidia** announced Monday it will invest **$3.5 billion** in **MediaTek** through convertible bonds, deepening a partnership that now spans AI data-center infrastructure, consumer PCs, and automotive platforms, per Bloomberg and TechCrunch. The centerpiece is technical, not just financial: MediaTek will adopt Nvidia's **NVLink Fusion** platform, which lets hyperscalers, cloud providers, and model developers design custom accelerator chips that plug directly into Nvidia's rack-scale systems.

The logic is a calculated concession. A growing list of Nvidia's biggest customers—**Amazon**, **Google**, **Microsoft**, **OpenAI**, and **Anthropic** among them—are building their own chips to lean less on its GPUs, and NVLink Fusion is how Nvidia stays in the room anyway. By helping MediaTek serve that custom-silicon demand, Nvidia trades some GPU share for the more durable position of owning the interconnect and data-center scaffolding everything else connects into. The deal also extends existing work on **RTX Spark** and **DGX Spark** chips for PCs and MediaTek's **Dimensity Auto** automotive line—another sign that Nvidia would rather partner with the challengers than be routed around by them.

### Tim Cook Works His Last Day as Apple CEO; John Ternus Takes Over September 1

After **15 years** running the company, **Tim Cook** spent Monday as **Apple's** CEO for the final time, with hardware-engineering chief **John Ternus** set to take the reins on **September 1**, per Bloomberg and 9to5Mac. Cook, who becomes executive chairman of Apple's board, grew Apple from a roughly **$350 billion** company when he succeeded Steve Jobs in 2011 into one that touched a **$5 trillion** market capitalization in July 2026. Ternus joined Apple in 2001 and was promoted to senior vice president of Hardware Engineering in 2021.

The handoff lands at an awkward moment. Ternus inherits a hardware franchise at its peak but an AI effort visibly behind, with **Apple Intelligence** underwhelming and the promised **Siri** overhaul delayed. His first major test comes fast: Apple is expected to hold its first event under the new CEO on **September 9**, when it will unveil the next iPhone and could reveal a long-rumored foldable. The choice of a hardware leader over a services or AI executive signals where Apple thinks its advantage still lies—even as the industry's center of gravity keeps shifting toward software and models.

### Anthropic Wins Court Ruling Ordering the Pentagon to Drop Its 'Supply Chain Risk' Label

A federal judge ordered the **Pentagon** to remove its designation of **Anthropic** as a "supply chain risk," ruling the label unlawful in a decision handed down late last week, per NPR, CNN, and Bloomberg. Judge **Rita Lin** found the action "constituted unlawful retaliation in violation of the First Amendment" and that Anthropic "was denied the pre-deprivation process required under the Fifth Amendment," concluding the company's products posed no meaningful national-security threat.

The dispute traces back to Anthropic's refusal to strip safety guardrails that block its **Claude** models from use in autonomous weapons and mass surveillance. Defense Secretary **Pete Hegseth** responded in February by branding the company a supply chain risk—a step that effectively barred it from federal contracts. Lin's ruling pointed to the government's own statements about Anthropic's "arrogance" as evidence the blacklisting was retaliation rather than a genuine security judgment. Beyond the company, the case is an early marker of how far the government can go in punishing AI firms for where they draw their safety lines.

## GNSS News

### TrustPoint and NovAtel Track C-Band Navigation Through GNSS Jamming for the U.S. Navy

The clearest positioning story of the week is a working alternative to the GPS signal itself. **TrustPoint** announced it demonstrated its **C-band** navigation signals operating through GNSS interference on an enhanced receiver built by **Hexagon's NovAtel**, under a contract with the **U.S. Naval Air Systems Command (NAVAIR)**, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. In the test, resilient positioning data was acquired, tracked, and generated on the NovAtel receiver while conventional GNSS signals were being jammed—what the companies called an industry first for GNSS-independent navigation on third-party hardware.

The significance is that it runs on standard gear. Much of 2026's GNSS-denied work has leaned on bespoke sensors or specialized platforms, but proving a commercial C-band LEO-PNT signal can be picked up by an existing receiver design lowers the bar for fielding it widely across military and eventually commercial users. C-band sits at a higher frequency than legacy GPS, making the signals harder to overpower, and pairing TrustPoint's transmissions with a mainstream receiver maker like NovAtel is how an alternative signal moves from lab demonstration toward something integrators can actually adopt. It advances the same goal driving the rest of the field: keeping platforms navigating when the primary satellite link is denied.

### ESA Backs onocoy's AI to Tell Spoofed GNSS Signals From Real Ones

On the detection side, the **European Space Agency** is funding new ways to catch interference before it does damage. In an ESA-supported project, **onocoy** simulated, collected, and analyzed spoofed GNSS data to train artificial intelligence to distinguish faked signals from genuine ones, with later phases aimed at developing, validating, and deploying the fraud-detection algorithms across onocoy's distributed monitoring network, per ESA and GPS World. Jamming drowns out satellite signals with stronger noise; spoofing is subtler, feeding a receiver false position and timing that it accepts as real.

The stakes are economic as much as technical. ESA notes that roughly **10%** of European GDP is enabled by satellite navigation, and that more than **10,000** ships were reported affected by malicious interference in a single quarter of 2025—an eightfold jump from the quarter before. Rather than harden every receiver, the approach spreads detection across a crowdsourced network of monitoring stations and lets AI flag anomalies in real time, turning a distributed sensor grid into an early-warning system. It complements the resilience push elsewhere in the industry: where some are building signals that resist attack, ESA is backing tools that simply notice when an attack is underway.
