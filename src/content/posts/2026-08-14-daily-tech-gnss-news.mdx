Friday's tech tape had a common thread running through it: China. Apple went its own way on AI for the mainland, a Chinese foundry showed just how much the AI boom is lifting local chipmakers, and a Chinese robotaxi maker booked a path into Europe through Uber. The counter-note was valuation gravity—one of the best-run names in chip equipment posted record numbers and still fell, because "great" no longer clears a bar set for "extraordinary." In positioning, the news moved from headlines about a jammed sky to the quieter work of building resilience into the machines that depend on it—tractors and combat vehicles alike.

## Tech News

### Apple Trains Its Own China AI Model With Alibaba—A Regulatory First

**Apple** has developed a China-specific large language model with help from **Alibaba**, a notable shift for a company that had leaned on local third-party models to power AI features for Chinese users, per Reuters as reported by Yahoo Finance and 9to5Mac. The bigger deal is the permission: Apple becomes the first foreign company Beijing has cleared to deploy its own proprietary AI model in China, a milestone at a moment of deep US–China friction over artificial intelligence. China's Cyberspace Administration registered Apple's generative-AI service last month, clearing the path.

The arrangement is a hybrid. Apple gets more control over the experience by training its own model, while Alibaba's **Qwen** stays integrated into **Apple Intelligence** across iOS, iPadOS, macOS and visionOS for Chinese users to satisfy local requirements, per the coverage. The strategic read is that Apple is trying to counter Huawei's homegrown AI push on its most important non-US market, and Apple Intelligence is now expected to reach Chinese iPhones in the coming months via a software update. For a company that has moved cautiously on AI everywhere, doing something first in China—of all markets—is the surprise.

### Applied Materials Posts Record Revenue—and Falls Anyway

**Applied Materials** reported fiscal third-quarter revenue of **$9.12 billion**, up **24.8%** year over year, with adjusted earnings of **$3.50** per share beating the **$3.40** consensus and gross margin at **50.4%** adjusted, per Benzinga and Yahoo Finance. The chip-equipment maker guided Q4 to **$10.25 billion** (plus or minus $500 million), up **51%** year over year, with non-GAAP EPS of **$4.02**, up **85%**—and CEO **Gary Dickerson** described "unprecedented" demand visibility heading into another strong 2027. Yet the stock fell.

The drop is the story. After a roughly **200%** run into the print, a record quarter and a 51%-growth guide still weren't enough to clear expectations already priced for perfection—a warning that echoes across the semiconductor supply chain, where strong is no longer sufficient when valuations assume years of extraordinary AI spending. There was a genuine mix shift underneath, too: China's share of revenue eased to **28%** from **35%** as the US nearly doubled its contribution, per the earnings coverage, trimming Applied's exposure to the geopolitical risk that has shadowed the group.

### Uber and Pony.ai Plan 2,000 Robotaxis for Europe

**Uber** and Chinese autonomous-vehicle maker **Pony.ai** are expanding their partnership to deploy more than **2,000 robotaxis** across Europe, per TechCrunch and CNBC. The rollout builds on Europe's first commercial robotaxi service in **Zagreb**—launched with Croatian mobility company **Verne** as local fleet operator—and will extend to additional European cities in phases, with the Middle East also in the plans. The tie-up dates to May 2025, when the two first agreed to bring Pony.ai vehicles onto Uber's platform in international markets.

The companies didn't name the cities or give a timeline, saying details will come in stages, per TechCrunch—so this is direction more than schedule. Still, it's a meaningful marker: a Chinese AV developer scaling into Europe on the back of the West's largest ride-hailing network, and a reminder that the robotaxi race is now as much about distribution partnerships as about the self-driving stack itself.

## GNSS News

### Tersus Ships an Autosteer Kit That Keeps Guiding When the Signal Drops

The most practical positioning story of the week is about a tractor that doesn't lose its line. **Tersus GNSS** launched the **AG993**, a modular autosteer retrofit kit that pairs high-precision GNSS with the company's proprietary **Tersus Advanced Positioning (TAP)** L-band satellite-correction service alongside conventional RTK, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. It's rated to better than **2.5 cm** accuracy across a **0.2–30 km/h** working range (down to 0.1 km/h), with automatic headland U-turns, and Tersus says it fits more than **90%** of agricultural vehicles across brands.

The clever bit is the fallback. If an RTK correction stream is interrupted—cellular dead zone, base-station dropout—the kit's **TAPFill** function automatically switches to the L-band TAP signal while holding the same RTK coordinate reference frame, so guidance continues without a jump, per the coverage. It supports GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou, QZSS, SBAS and IRNSS. Where most of this year's PNT news has been about defending signals in contested airspace, this is resilience for the field: a farmer's autosteer that keeps working when the correction link—not the satellites—goes quiet, which is the failure mode precision agriculture actually hits.

### Pacific Defense Wins an Army Card for Navigating a GPS-Denied Battlefield

On the defense side, the theme is standards-based, GPS-denied navigation moving into fielded hardware. **Pacific Defense** won a U.S. **Army PM PNT** contract to develop a **CMOSS Mounted Form Factor (CMFF)** Assured PNT **Block 2** plug-in card, per Inside GNSS and GPS World. The rugged **3U VPX** card is aligned to the open **SOSA** and **CMOSS** standards and fuses GPS, alternative navigation signals, inertial sensors and platform vehicle data to hold position and timing when traditional GNSS is jammed or spoofed.

The open-architecture angle is what matters. By building to SOSA/CMOSS, the Army can slot resilient PNT into vehicles as a standard card rather than a bespoke box—easier to upgrade and swap as threats evolve. Pacific Defense will enhance its Block 1 design to CMFF requirements and deliver prototypes for integration and test, per the coverage. It's the ground-vehicle counterpart to the airborne resilience work that has dominated the year: making assured positioning a modular, replaceable component of the platform rather than a one-off retrofit.
