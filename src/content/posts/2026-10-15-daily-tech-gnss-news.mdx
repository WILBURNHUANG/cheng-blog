Thursday's news undid Tuesday's chip-sector gloom in a single session. The foundry at the center of the AI buildout reported its strongest quarter yet, the one indispensable equipment supplier confirmed demand it cannot keep up with, and the chip stocks that had sold off on fears of a Chinese challenger rallied back. Underneath the day's rebound ran a quieter contest over who controls the AI data center, with Nvidia answering the hyperscalers' move to build their own silicon by opening its rack designs and tightening its grip on the network that ties the chips together. In positioning, the steady lesson held once more, expressed this time as two different ways to reach beyond GPS: a jamming-resistant new frequency band, and a constellation that turns satellite signals into a weather-sensing instrument.

## Tech News

### TSMC Posts a Sixth Straight Record Quarter, Sparking a Chip Rally

The day's defining story was the clearest answer yet to the question hanging over the sector: is the AI demand real. **TSMC** reported third-quarter revenue of **NT$1.494 trillion**, up **50.9%** year over year and **17.6%** from the prior quarter—its **sixth consecutive** record quarter—ahead of its October 15 earnings conference, per coverage citing the company's figures. High-performance computing, the segment that captures AI accelerators and data-center processors, has been running near **two-thirds** of revenue, with demand concentrated in **3-nanometer** process technology and **CoWoS** advanced packaging, the capacity bottleneck TSMC has been racing to expand for its largest customers, **Nvidia** and **Apple**.

The market read it as reassurance. US-listed chip names rallied and the major indices rose as TSMC's results "boosted AI hopes," according to market coverage from Yahoo Finance and Reuters, with banks adding to the gains on their own earnings. The timing mattered as much as the numbers: it landed barely a day after a memory-led selloff dragged Asian indices to multi-month lows on fears China's CXMT was closing the DRAM gap. TSMC's record quarter did not resolve that worry, but it reminded investors that the company actually manufacturing the AI era's most advanced silicon is still selling everything it can make.

### Update: ASML's Actual Q3 Comes In Hot, and It Can't Make Enough for 2027-2028

The preview became a result, and the result was stronger than the guidance implied. **ASML** reported **€11.7 billion** in third-quarter net sales—toward the high end of its own range—with net income of **€5.0 billion**, a gross margin of **57.5%**, and basic earnings of **€12.95** per share, per the company's October 14 release. The number that moved the debate, though, was the forecast: ASML guided fourth-quarter net sales to **€14.2 billion to €15.2 billion**, a sharp step up, and raised its full-year outlook to **€44 billion to €45 billion**.

The genuinely new signal was on supply. CEO **Christophe Fouquet** said ASML "now expect[s] that our supply as currently planned for both 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers," adding that some customers are already interested in placing orders for **2029 and even 2030**. The company plans a **30%** capacity increase in 2027 and is investigating a further **30%** for 2028. After a month in which chip executives warned that capital, not supply, was the binding constraint, the monopoly supplier at the front of the chain just said the opposite about its own machines—it cannot build them fast enough.

### Nvidia Answers the In-House Chip Wave by Opening Its Architecture

The sharper contest ran beneath the rally. Days after Microsoft unveiled its Maia 300 and AMD open-sourced a rival rack design, **Nvidia** used the **OCP Global Summit** in San Jose on **October 12** to make a counter-move: it contributed its **Vera Rubin** rack and compute-tray designs to the Open Compute Project through its open **MGX** architecture, per Nvidia. The updated **MGX 1.2** spec adds rack-scale blind-mate liquid cooling, a liquid-cooled 5,000-amp busbar, and deployments up to **250 kilowatts**—publishing, rather than guarding, the blueprint the hyperscalers want to build to.

The deeper play was the network. **Meta** said it is adopting Nvidia's **Spectrum-6** Ethernet switches at **102.4 Tb/s**—double the capacity of the prior generation—to scale fabrics beyond **100,000** GPUs, while **Google** will support its **Falcon Transport** on future Nvidia **ConnectX-10** SuperNICs and BlueField DPUs. Nvidia also co-authored open data-center and grid-resilience specifications with **AMD, Google, Meta, and Microsoft**, including a standard for restoring AI infrastructure to near-full load in under **two seconds** after a grid disturbance. The message is that even as its biggest customers design their own accelerators to escape its pricing, Nvidia intends to remain the connective tissue of the data center—and to set the standards everyone else builds against.

## GNSS News

### TrustPoint and Calian Demonstrate the First Mass-Market-Ready C-Band GNSS Antenna

The freshest positioning news was about opening a new, harder-to-jam frequency for everyday use. **TrustPoint**, a C-band GNSS provider, announced on **October 14** that antenna maker **Calian** had demonstrated what it calls the industry's first commercially manufacturable **C-band** GNSS antenna, which consistently acquired and tracked TrustPoint's live C-band signal from orbit across several test passes, per Inside GNSS. The companies frame C-band as a complement to legacy GPS that offers greater jamming resistance, smaller antennas, lower ionospheric error, and better availability in cities—an independent layer in a resilient positioning, navigation, and timing stack.

The move matters because resilience only scales if the hardware can be mass-produced. TrustPoint is building a **four-satellite, four-ground-station** system under a US Space Force Tactical Funding Increase contract, with live trilateration and initial services targeted within **12 months**, and has also shown C-band positioning on **NovAtel** receivers in GPS-denied conditions. A multi-band antenna spanning L1, L5, and C-band is now in development with Calian. "C-band has already proven its value across numerous industries and applications," said TrustPoint founder and CEO **Patrick Shannon**, while Calian's **Christopher Russell** called extending the firm's antenna expertise to C-band "a natural next step"—the signal that a GPS alternative is moving from demonstration toward a producible product.

### PlanetiQ Launches GNOMES-10 to Expand Its GNSS Radio-Occultation Weather Constellation

The complementary story used GNSS signals not to position anything but to read the atmosphere. **PlanetiQ** said on **October 14** that its **GNOMES-10** satellite, launched **October 1** on SpaceX's Transporter-18 mission from Vandenberg Space Force Base, had made first contact and begun commissioning, per a PR Newswire release. The satellite carries the company's fourth-generation **Pyxis** GNSS radio-occultation receiver, which measures how satellite signals bend through Earth's atmosphere to resolve atmospheric and ionospheric structure—data that feeds weather forecasting and space-weather monitoring.

The significance is in the density of observation. The Pyxis receiver tracks **GPS, BeiDou, GLONASS, and Galileo** simultaneously across a **±75-degree** limb-sounding field of view, which PlanetiQ says yields more radio-occultation soundings per hour than rival commercial constellations, including in the hard-to-measure lower troposphere. A twin satellite, **GNOMES-11**, is set to launch later this month to add lower-inclination coverage. "We are proud that our data supports weather applications by measuring Earth's atmospheric and ionospheric state," said founder and chairman **Chris McCormick**—a reminder that the same signals the day's other GNSS story is working to protect have become a sensing instrument in their own right.
