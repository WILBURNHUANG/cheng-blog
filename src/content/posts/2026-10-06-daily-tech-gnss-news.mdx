Monday's news offered a rare break from the all-AI-infrastructure script, even as the machinery underneath it kept setting records. The day's biggest deal was a European industrial giant paying a premium for software rather than silicon—a reminder that the physical-world economy is doing its own AI shopping. The hardware demand was still there in the numbers, with a contract manufacturer clearing its first-ever trillion-NT-dollar month, and the consumer side of AI took a turn toward paying its own way. In positioning, the year's steady lesson held once more: build for the day the satellite signal is jammed, spoofed, or simply gone. Two milestones this week did exactly that—one encrypting the signal itself, one giving the nation's clocks a second source.

## Tech News

### Schneider Electric Buys PTC for $22.6 Billion in Its Largest-Ever Deal

The day's defining move spent big on software for the physical world. **Schneider Electric** agreed to acquire **PTC**, the Boston-based industrial-software maker, for **$22.6 billion**—**$205** per share, a **42.3%** premium to PTC's prior close and an enterprise value near **$23.7 billion**, per Tech Startups. The French energy-management group will fund the deal with roughly **€5–6 billion** in new equity and **€16–17 billion** in new debt, with the purchase expected to close in **Q3 2027**; Schneider's own shares fell nearly **10%** on the size of the bill.

The logic runs through the factory floor rather than the data center. PTC's design and lifecycle tools—CAD, PLM, and industrial IoT—sit at the heart of how manufacturers model and run physical products, and Schneider is betting that pairing them with its automation and energy hardware creates a stickier industrial-software stack as factories digitize. The market's flinch at the price is the familiar tension of 2026's deal spree: buyers are paying rich premiums to own strategic software, and investors are increasingly asking whether the returns will justify the debt. That the biggest acquisition of the day was about industrial software rather than AI accelerators is itself a small signal that the spending is broadening out.

### Foxconn Posts Its First Trillion-NT-Dollar Month as AI Hardware Demand Surges

The clearest proof of underlying demand came from the company that assembles it. **Foxconn** reported third-quarter revenue of **NT$3.03 trillion** (about **$95.4 billion**), up **47%** year over year and ahead of the **NT$2.83 trillion** analysts expected, with **September** alone bringing in **NT$1.16 trillion**—a **38%** jump and the first time the manufacturer has cleared **NT$1 trillion** in a single month, per Tech Startups.

The read is that the AI-server buildout is still translating into real orders, not just announcements. Foxconn is the primary assembler of the AI servers and systems hyperscalers are racing to deploy, so its revenue is a cleaner demand gauge than any single chipmaker's guidance. A record quarter capped by a record month says the capital companies keep committing to AI capacity is landing on the factory floor as actual hardware—the tangible other side of a year otherwise dominated by financing structures and paper commitments.

### OpenAI Starts Testing Ads Inside ChatGPT's 1.2-Billion-User Image Generator

The week's other OpenAI story was about money, not safety. **OpenAI** began testing **visual ads** inside **ChatGPT's** image-generation feature for US users in **October**, its first real move to monetize a free product that now reaches roughly **1.2 billion** weekly users, per Tech Startups. The company said paid tiers—**Plus, Pro, and Enterprise**—will not show ads.

The significance is the pivot it signals. OpenAI has carried enormous compute costs against a user base that is overwhelmingly free, and advertising is the classic way a consumer platform at billion-user scale closes that gap. Putting ads in image generation first—where a branded product can appear natively inside what the user asked to create—tests whether AI-native advertising can work without the backlash that greets ads bolted onto a chat window. For a company spending this month on safety crises and compute financing, it is a reminder that the other half of the equation, revenue, is still being invented.

## GNSS News

### Galileo Broadcasts an Encrypted Signal That Lets Civil Receivers Authenticate Their Position

The freshest positioning news was a signal that fights back against spoofing. At **Jammertest 2026**—the annual week-long interference exercise held in mid-September on the Arctic island of **Andøya, Norway**—Europe's **Galileo** system achieved the first civil authenticated position fix using its new **Signal Authentication Service (SAS)**, with five operational Galileo satellites broadcasting a new encrypted signal that ground receivers used to establish a verified position on **September 16**, per GPS World. The **European Space Agency** led the testing alongside partners including **Septentrio, u-blox, Kongsberg Discovery, and Leonardo**, collecting dozens of terabytes of data for months of post-processing.

The milestone matters because it attacks spoofing at the source rather than after the fact. Most anti-spoofing today is detection—spotting when a position looks wrong—but an authenticated signal lets a receiver cryptographically confirm that the ranging data actually came from the satellite, shutting the door on faked signals before they corrupt a fix. Demonstrating it live under the realistic jamming and spoofing conditions Jammertest is built to create, rather than in a lab, is the step that moves signal authentication from specification toward something receiver makers can build around. Against the year's steady push to treat GNSS as deniable, it is the complementary move: making the signal itself harder to fake.

### Oscilloquartz Extends Iridium's Satellite Timing Across Its Clock Portfolio

The complementary story came from the timing side, where GPS dependence is most systemic and least visible. On **September 28**, **Oscilloquartz**, the synchronization arm of **Adtran**, expanded support for **Iridium's PNT** services across its full lineup of grandmaster clocks and timing servers—**ruggedSync, edgeSync, accessSync**, and its **OSAinside** embedded products—and became an authorized Iridium reseller, per Inside GNSS. Iridium PNT is the low-Earth-orbit **Satellite Time and Location** service Iridium acquired with Satelles in 2024, and the expansion spans all three tiers: **PNT Time, Locate, and Navigate**.

The move matters because it makes a GPS backup something operators can adopt without rebuilding. Critical infrastructure—mobile networks, financial systems, power grids—leans heavily on GPS-derived timing, the single most jamming-exposed dependency in the PNT stack. By building Iridium's stronger, harder-to-jam LEO timing signal into the same grandmaster clocks telecom and utility customers already run, Oscilloquartz is pushing layered, multi-source timing into existing deployments rather than asking for a forklift upgrade. Coming days after Iridium moved its PNT chip into commercial production, it signals that satellite-timing resilience is shifting from a specialized add-on toward a default feature of the equipment that keeps critical networks in sync.
