Friday's news kept circling one shift: the AI boom is moving out of the cloud and into the physical economy—power gear, foundries, supply chains, and who finances them. The Pentagon floated a loan bigger than anything its investment arm has done, aimed not at a data center but at the parts that build one. Broadcom and TSMC both showed the demand is booked, not hoped for. And China's markets kept minting chip champions overnight. In positioning, the throughline was the same instinct running all week—don't trust a single signal—expressed two ways: a startup building its own navigation constellation from scratch, and a navy learning to keep time without satellites at all.

## Tech News

### Broadcom's AI Chip Revenue Triples to $16.7 Billion as It Guides to $230 Billion by 2028

**Broadcom** reported a record fiscal third quarter, with AI semiconductor revenue more than tripling year over year to **$16.7 billion** and total revenue up **86%** to **$29.6 billion**, per its earnings call coverage on The Motley Fool and Investing.com. The company raised its fiscal-2026 AI revenue target to **$58 billion** and laid out formal long-term goals of roughly **$115 billion** in fiscal 2027 and **$230 billion** in fiscal 2028—guidance that reframes Broadcom from a diversified chip-and-software house into one of the AI buildout's central arms suppliers.

The engine is custom silicon. Broadcom said shipments of its **XPU** accelerators—built for **Google, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta**—climbed more than **3.5x** year over year and now make up about **73%** of AI revenue. OpenAI's custom "Jalapeño" accelerator began shipping in the quarter, with a deployment roadmap stretching past **5 gigawatts** in 2028. The message underneath the numbers is that the hyperscalers' push to design their own chips isn't a threat to Broadcom—it's Broadcom's business, and it is compounding.

### TSMC Posts Its First-Ever NT$500 Billion Month as AI Demand Outruns Supply

**Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.** reported August revenue of **NT$514.8 billion (about $16.3 billion)**, up **53.3%** year over year and **10.1%** from July—the first time the world's largest contract chipmaker has cleared NT$500 billion in a single month, and its fourth straight month of growth, per CNBC and Bloomberg. TSMC has repeatedly described AI-related demand as "extremely robust," and its most advanced 5-, 4-, and 3-nanometer nodes ran at full capacity through the quarter on orders for AI server chips.

The monthly print has become one of the cleaner real-world reads on whether the AI hardware cycle is still accelerating, and August says it is—demand continues to outstrip what even TSMC can supply. With a foundry market share around **72%**, the company sits at a chokepoint that every AI ambition ultimately runs through: Nvidia, Broadcom's XPUs, and the custom accelerators the hyperscalers keep announcing all get fabricated in the same Taiwanese cleanrooms.

### The Pentagon Weighs a $5 Billion Loan to Wire the Data-Center Supply Chain Into Industrial Strategy

The **Pentagon** is in talks to lend roughly **$5 billion** to AI cloud-computing startup **Fluidstack** to shore up the U.S. data-center supply chain, per a Wall Street Journal report carried by Reuters. The money would come from the Defense Department's **Office of Strategic Capital**—and at $5 billion would be by far its largest loan to date. Notably, it isn't earmarked for a new AI facility but for building domestic manufacturing capacity for the **power and cooling equipment** that determines how fast AI infrastructure can actually be stood up.

The framing is the story. It treats the bottleneck in AI—increasingly power gear and thermal hardware, not just GPUs—as a matter of national industrial capacity rather than private capital allocation. It lands alongside a recent executive order declaring a national emergency and restricting some foreign equipment in the U.S. electricity grid, and it signals that Washington now views the physical plumbing of AI as strategic infrastructure worth underwriting directly.

## GNSS News

### TrustPoint Wins a Space Force Contract to Mass-Produce 40 GPS-Independent Satellites

The sharpest "build a second signal" story came from an orbit-up bet. **SpaceWERX**—the innovation arm of the U.S. Space Force, within AFWERX—awarded **TrustPoint** and **EnduroSat US** a contract to build **40 satellites** for TrustPoint's GPS-independent positioning, navigation and timing constellation, delivered in **three tranches**, per GPS World. TrustPoint, which broadcasts a **C-band** satellite-navigation signal from low-Earth orbit, has already launched and operated three free-flying satellites carrying its payload; this award moves it from demonstrated flight heritage to constellation-scale, full-rate production.

The thesis behind the deal is that resilient PNT no longer requires a multi-billion-dollar bespoke constellation. Partnering with EnduroSat—a volume manufacturer trusted by NASA and ESA—lets TrustPoint apply lessons from each tranche to the next in the same capital-efficient, iterate-fast model it has used since its first launch. It builds on a run of milestones including the first live-sky transmission and real-time reception of a C-band GNSS signal, a demonstration of those signals surviving interference on a **Hexagon** receiver, and a Space Force **TACFI** award to field a four-satellite, four-ground-station GPS-independent system. As CEO Patrick Shannon put it, the demand for resilient precision PNT "isn't ordinary market demand, it's urgent, and for some users, existential."

### At Sea, the UK Keeps Testing Quantum Clocks as a GNSS Backup

While TrustPoint builds a new signal, a parallel effort assumes there may be no usable signal at all. In a GPS World feature on resilient maritime positioning, quantum-sensing startup **Aquark Technologies** detailed its trial of **AQlock**—described as the UK's first industrially designed and built **cold-atom clock**—which ran continuously for three days aboard the Royal Navy P2000 patrol vessel **HMS Pursuer** in the Solent. Cold-atom clocks and sensors measure time and motion off stable atomic properties rather than aging mechanical parts, so they drift far less than the inertial systems ships fall back on when satellites go dark—a growing need given persistent jamming and spoofing in chokepoints like the **Strait of Hormuz**.

The point is timing as much as position: a clock that holds its accuracy independently of GNSS gives a vessel a stable reference to navigate from, and combined with quantum inertial sensing it can keep a solution bounded for far longer at sea. The UK's **HARLEQUIN** project—led by CPI TMD with the University of Strathclyde, Covesion, and Trinity House—recently ran the first field demonstration of a cold-atom navigation system built on grating magneto-optical trap technology, pairing a ring-laser gyroscope and atomic clock with a quantum accelerometer. None of it is ready to replace GNSS outright, but the direction is clear: resilience is coming from a system-of-systems where satellite, inertial, and quantum sources cover for one another.

## Key Takeaways

- **The AI buildout is becoming industrial policy, not just a spending race**: The Pentagon opened talks on a $5 billion loan—its investment arm's largest ever—to help a cloud startup build domestic power-and-cooling manufacturing, treating the physical plumbing of AI as strategic infrastructure worth underwriting directly.
- **The chip supply chain is booked, and the money knows it**: Broadcom's AI revenue tripled to $16.7 billion with guidance to $230 billion by 2028, TSMC posted its first-ever NT$500 billion month on demand it still can't fully meet, and Tencent-backed Enflame tripled in a Shanghai debut that valued it near $25.5 billion.
- **GNSS resilience is being built from orbit down and the atom up**: TrustPoint won a Space Force contract to mass-produce 40 GPS-independent C-band satellites, while the UK kept trialing cold-atom clocks aboard a Royal Navy vessel—two bets that the answer to an untrustworthy signal is either a new one or none at all.
