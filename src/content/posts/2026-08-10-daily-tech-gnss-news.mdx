Monday was about who pays for the AI buildout and who controls the silicon underneath it. Intel went to the market for cash to fund its comeback, Microsoft moved to make more of its own chips, and Meta handed the buildout back to everyone by open-sourcing an agent model small enough to run on a gaming PC. Stocks, for their part, sat on their hands ahead of an inflation report that could reset the rate-cut math. In positioning, the through-line ran below the signal itself—Europe pushing to rebuild satellite navigation around lasers, and the U.S. military formally taking the keys to its next-generation GPS gear.

## Tech News

### Intel Launches a $15 Billion Stock Sale—Its First Since 1971—to Fund the Foundry Comeback

**Intel** announced a proposed **$15 billion** common-stock offering on Monday, seizing on a near three-fold run in its shares this year to bankroll its foundry expansion and next-generation manufacturing, per Bloomberg, SiliconANGLE, and Intel's own newsroom. It is the chipmaker's first public share sale since it listed in **1971**—a striking move for a company that spent much of the past two years fighting doubts about its turnaround. A **$2.25 billion** greenshoe option could push the raise higher still.

The money is pointed squarely at capacity. Intel said proceeds go to general corporate purposes including capital expenditure and working capital, layered on top of a capex budget running as high as **$20 billion** this year, with the cash underwriting its **14A** process node and advanced-packaging build-out—the technologies it's betting will win back leading-edge foundry customers amid AI-driven demand, per SiliconANGLE and MLQ. Coverage noted the raise leans on the strength of a rally that has roughly tripled the stock in 2026. Selling equity into that surge is the tell: after years of borrowing and asset sales, Intel is now confident enough in the demand signal to fund the comeback with its own shares.

### Microsoft Moves to Make Its Own AI Chips at Scale, Booking 300,000 Maia 300 Units at TSMC

**Microsoft** is preparing to "significantly" ramp production of its custom AI silicon, holding talks with **Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.** to secure capacity for more than **300,000** of its next-generation **Maia 300** accelerators for delivery in **2027**, per Bloomberg (citing The Information) and Yahoo Finance. The company is expected to unveil the chip this fall—reportedly as soon as September—with a longer-term ambition of eventually producing north of **one million** units.

The strategic point is independence. Maia is the datacenter silicon Microsoft uses to lower the cost of running its own cloud AI—services like Foundry and Copilot—and a 300,000-chip order signals a real push to shift AI inference off externally purchased GPUs and onto hardware it controls across Azure, per The Next Web and Techzine. Microsoft is even said to be courting **Anthropic** to run on the accelerator. The caveat matters: Microsoft, TSMC, and Anthropic have not confirmed the volumes, timeline, or specs, and the reporting rests on people familiar with the plans. But the direction is unmistakable—like Intel's raise, it's another major player deciding the AI era is worth building its own chips for rather than renting someone else's.

### Meta Open-Sources Muse Glimmer, a 30B Agent Model That Runs on a Single GPU

**Meta** released **Muse Glimmer** on Monday, a **30-billion-parameter** open-weights model built specifically to run autonomous agent workflows offline on a single consumer GPU, published under the Apache 2.0 license on Hugging Face by Meta Superintelligence Labs, per MarkTechPost, Phoronix, and Open Source For You. Through 4-bit quantization, Meta compressed the model's memory footprint from roughly **55 GB** down to **18–20 GB**, letting the whole stack—model, KV cache, perception encoder, and speculative-decoding drafter—fit inside a **24 GB** VRAM envelope on a single PC or Mac.

The design goal is what makes it notable: always-on, local agents that handle coding, function calling, and multi-step reasoning without a round trip to the cloud. Meta distilled Glimmer from a larger frontier model that stays closed and metered at **$4.25 per million output tokens** through its paid API, per the coverage—so the company is giving away the compact, on-device workhorse while keeping the cloud-scale brain behind a paywall. Set against Intel and Microsoft racing to control the hardware layer, Glimmer is a bet on the opposite frontier: pushing capable agents down onto commodity silicon that anyone already owns.

## GNSS News

### ESA Pushes OpSTAR Toward Contract, Betting Lasers Can Re-Architect Satellite Navigation

The **European Space Agency** is moving its **OpSTAR** (Optical Synchronised Time and Ranging) demonstrator from study into build, inviting industry in the second half of 2026 to bid on the implementation phase of an in-orbit mission designed to test whether optical technology can rework the foundations of satellite navigation, per Inside GNSS and ESA. Related ESA navigation tenders are closing through mid-August, with the OpSTAR-linked opportunity extended to **August 17**.

The concept is a genuine departure from how GNSS works today. Rather than lean on radio signals, OpSTAR would use **inter-satellite laser links** to let satellites autonomously synchronize their clocks, determine their orbits with high precision, and exchange PNT data both between spacecraft and down to the ground—optical links that are far harder to jam than radiofrequency, per ESA. Funded as one of three missions under the **FutureNAV** program at the 2024 Ministerial Council and studied by a consortium led by Germany's **OHB System**, OpSTAR aims at the resilience problem from an unusual angle: not adding another signal in a contested band, but changing the medium so interference has far less to grab onto. Where much of this year's PNT news has been about backing up GPS, this is about redrawing the architecture underneath it.

### The Air Force Formally Takes Ownership of the Military's Next-Gen GPS Gear

On the defense side, the **Air Force Life Cycle Management Center** (AFLCMC) has signed the **Military GPS User Equipment (MGUE) Transition Support Plan**, formally accepting the MGUE **Increment 1 and 2** programs from the U.S. Space Force and finalizing the Air Force's full ownership of the two navigation-modernization efforts, per Inside GNSS. MGUE is the receiver-side half of GPS modernization—the hardware that lets aircraft, ground vehicles, and weapons actually use the encrypted, jam-resistant **M-code** signal that modernized GPS satellites broadcast.

The handoff is a bureaucratic milestone with an operational point. M-code is the military's answer to exactly the jamming and spoofing that has plagued civil navigation this year, but the signal is only as useful as the fielded equipment that can process it—and getting that user equipment through development, integration, and into platforms has long been the slow link in the chain. Transferring lifecycle ownership to AFLCMC consolidates responsibility for pushing MGUE into the Air Force's fleets, a quiet but necessary step toward turning modernized GPS from a capability on orbit into one soldiers and pilots can rely on in a contested environment.
