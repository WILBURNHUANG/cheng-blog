Tuesday's session was the rebound finding a second leg—and a new engine. Where oil at multi-month highs had been the week's main drag, crude fell as Middle East peace talks drew attention, chips rose for a third straight day, and a jump in consumer confidence added to the mood, even as market breadth stayed stubbornly thin. The loudest single-stock story cut the other way, with Boeing tumbling on a deep quarterly miss despite a record order book. In positioning, the month's alternative-PNT thread kept moving from the lab toward the field: a programmable navigation platform aimed at developers, and an inertial navigator crossing over from military aircraft to a commercial airliner.

## Tech News

### Oil Reverses on Middle East Peace Talks as the Rebound Broadens

The October 27 session put a new driver under the recovery. The **Nasdaq Composite** rose **1.18%** to **26,477.81**, the **S&P 500** gained **0.60%** to **7,722.60**—within about **1.2%** of its record—and the **Dow** was essentially flat, up **0.03%** to **52,377.57**, while the **Russell 2000** added **1.16%** to **2,818.87**, per cappnotes and Investrade. Chips led for a third straight day, with the **Philadelphia Semiconductor Index** up **3.60%** behind **AMD** (**+5.45%**) and **Broadcom** (**+4.00%**).

The genuinely new element was energy. After two weeks in which oil at multi-month highs was the tape's main drag, crude fell as talks in Tehran among **Iran, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan** drew attention—**WTI** settled at **$99.90** and **Brent** at **$113.88**, making energy the day's worst sector at **-2.05%**, though a Houthi strike on Saudi Arabia's **Ras Tanura** refinery kept a supply premium alive. Consumer confidence reinforced the tone, with the **Conference Board** index jumping to **91.7** in October against an estimate near **81**. The caveat was breadth: the equal-weight S&P 500 rose just **0.11%** and the Nasdaq logged **239** new lows against **62** new highs, a sign the gains remain concentrated in a handful of large names.

### Boeing Tumbles Nearly 11% on a Q3 Miss and a 737 Production Warning

The day's biggest single-stock move was a deep miss from an industrial bellwether. **Boeing** shares fell **10.69%** after posting a third-quarter GAAP loss of **$0.26** a share—a core loss of **$0.34** against a **$0.20** estimate—on revenue of **$24.19 billion**, per Investrade. The **Commercial Airplanes** unit booked a **$697 million** operating loss at a **-6.5%** margin on **$10.7 billion** of revenue, and management warned it would come in "shy" of its **737** production target, pulling suppliers lower with it.

The paradox is that demand has rarely looked stronger. Boeing disclosed a record backlog of roughly **$745 billion**, underscoring that its problem is execution on the factory floor rather than orders. For a name that has spent years trying to steady its production system, the quarter was a reminder that the turnaround is still a manufacturing story first—and that the gap between a full order book and deliverable airplanes is where the losses still live.

### Celestica's AI-Server Boom Headlines a Beat-Heavy Earnings Tape

Beneath the macro, the quarter's prints kept showing the AI buildout pulling demand through to the companies that assemble it. Contract manufacturer **Celestica** reported third-quarter adjusted EPS of **$3.30**, ahead of the **$2.99** estimate, on revenue of **$5.83 billion** versus **$5.48 billion** expected, and lifted its **2026** revenue outlook to **$21.0 billion** from **$20.5 billion**—a direct read on surging demand for AI server and networking hardware, per Investrade's earnings roundup.

The broader tape leaned positive but uneven. **SoFi** jumped **13.4%** on record revenue and a strong 2027 outlook, **Corning** beat with core EPS of **$0.91**, and **UPS** and **Coca-Cola** both topped estimates, while **PayPal** slipped after revenue of **$8.58 billion** missed the **$8.71 billion** consensus even as it raised full-year EPS guidance to about **$5.60**. The split captured the moment: the companies closest to AI infrastructure are still setting the pace, while consumer- and payments-facing names are judged line by line.

## GNSS News

### Inertial Labs and VIAVI Launch a Programmable Alt-PNT Development Platform

The freshest positioning story moved resilient navigation onto the developer's bench. On **October 27**, **Inertial Labs**, now a **VIAVI Solutions** company, launched the **INS-NV**, a programmable platform for building real-time navigation and sensor-fusion systems that pairs an inertial navigation system and an attitude-and-heading reference system with an **NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX** edge-AI module, per Inside GNSS. The design lets engineers run their own machine-vision and visual-navigation software alongside Inertial Labs' INS and AHRS stack rather than integrating the pieces from scratch.

The hardware is built for GNSS-denied work. The INS-NV carries tactical-grade MEMS accelerometers and gyroscopes, a barometric altimeter and magnetometers, and a full-constellation GNSS receiver spanning **GPS, QZSS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou, and NavIC**, and ships with MAVLink, ASPN, pntOS, and MOSA-compliant variants on request—a nod to defense alt-PNT programs. "Visual navigation needs continuous information about vehicle position, orientation and time," said **Jamie Marraccini** of VIAVI, arguing the platform lets teams build alternative-PNT systems "without spending large R&D budgets on INS integration." It is the month's running lesson—survivable navigation needs a second source—recast as a toolkit for whoever wants to build one.

### Safran's SkyNaute Puts an Inertial Navigator on the Airbus A220

The complementary story marked a milestone for GNSS-independent navigation in civil aviation. **Airbus** has selected **Safran Electronics & Defense**'s **SkyNaute** inertial navigation system for the **A220**—the first time the system will fly on a commercial airliner, after service on military aircraft, helicopters, and drones across several European forces, per GPS World (**October 20**). SkyNaute fuses inertial sensing with hybridized GNSS data so it holds an accurate trajectory when satellite signals are weak, jammed, or spoofed.

At its core is Safran's **hemispherical resonator gyroscope (HRG)**, a patented design with no wearing mechanical parts that cuts weight, size, and power—practical advantages for airlines counting every kilogram. Demand is pulling investment with it: Safran committed **€120 million** in June to expand its **Montluçon** site and triple HRG output from **10,000** to **30,000** units a year by **2032**. "For the first time, our inertial navigation system will fly on an airliner," said **Benjamin Declety** of Safran's avionics unit—a sign that resilience features once reserved for defense are now being designed into the planes carrying passengers.
