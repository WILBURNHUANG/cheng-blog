The season's marquee earnings night finally arrived, and it delivered the cleanest illustration yet of the split running through the AI trade: one cloud giant proved the spending is working, the other showed how much it costs. Microsoft's Azure accelerated; Meta's margins buckled under a capex bill that keeps climbing. Layered on top was a Federal Reserve that refused to blink—holding rates while three officials pushed to raise them—and a market already jittery about chips that took the hawkish tone badly. In positioning, the mass-market GNSS world took its first serious public look at whether low-Earth-orbit signals belong in your phone.

## Tech News

### Microsoft Delivers, Meta Disappoints: One Earnings Night, Two AI Verdicts

The most-anticipated earnings session of the quarter split Big Tech in two on Wednesday. **Microsoft** reported fiscal fourth-quarter results with **Azure** cloud revenue up **43%**—its fastest growth in four years and comfortably ahead of its own **39–40%** guidance—as overall cloud revenue beat estimates, per Bloomberg. It was the clearest evidence so far this season that at least one hyperscaler's enormous AI buildout is translating into demand, with quarterly capital spending running above **$40 billion** and calendar-2026 outlays tracking toward roughly $190 billion.

**Meta** told the opposite story. Revenue rose **28%** to **$60.8 billion**, but net income of **$15.8 billion** and EPS of **$6.18** fell short as total expenses jumped **55%** to **$42.0 billion**, per CNBC, Variety, and Investing.com. Capital expenditures more than doubled year-over-year to **$31.1 billion** for the quarter, and Meta raised full-year 2026 capex guidance to **$130–145 billion**—up from $72.2 billion in 2025. The bottom line also absorbed roughly **$1.18 billion** in severance tied to the May layoff of about **8,000** workers. CEO **Mark Zuckerberg** defended the spend, saying "AI is accelerating our core business today," but investors focused on light revenue guidance and the lowest free cash flow in years, and the stock fell after hours. Same night, same industry, two answers to the question the market has been asking all month—does the AI spending pay off?

### The Fed Holds, but Three Dissents Point the Other Way

The **Federal Reserve** held its benchmark rate steady on Wednesday, but the decision landed as hawkish rather than reassuring: **three** members dissented in favor of a rate *hike*, an unusually sharp split that signaled the central bank is more worried about inflation than about cushioning a wobbling market, per Yahoo Finance and Bloomberg. Bond yields rose in response, and equities sold off hard—the **Dow** plunged more than **1,100 points**, the **S&P 500** fell about **1.5%**, and the **Nasdaq Composite** dropped roughly **1.7%**.

The timing amplified the pain. Traders were already dumping chip stocks ahead of the Big Tech prints, and a hawkish hold gave them one more reason to de-risk. Energy and technology were the day's worst-performing sectors while defensives like health care and consumer staples held up—the textbook rotation of a market bracing for higher-for-longer rates on top of AI-spending fatigue.

### Arm Tops Estimates, and Investors Shrug

**Arm Holdings** beat expectations in its latest quarter but couldn't get its stock to cooperate, with shares slipping after the report as the chip-design firm signaled continued sluggishness in the smartphone market, per Bloomberg. The muted reaction fit the week's mood exactly: in a tape this nervous, clearing the bar isn't enough—guidance and end-market health are what move the stock, and a soft read on phones was enough to overshadow the beat.

## GNSS News

### u-blox Takes the LEO-PNT Question to the Mass Market

The most consequential positioning story this week isn't a launch—it's a question being asked out loud by a company that ships chips by the millions. **u-blox** published a technical evaluation, conducted under **ESA's** Navigation Innovation and Support Programme (**NAVISP** Element 2), of how signals from low-Earth orbit could complement conventional GNSS in mass-market devices, per GPS World and Inside GNSS. The trigger was ESA's **Celeste** in-orbit demonstration: the first two satellites launched on **28 March 2026**, ESTEC received the first navigation signal on **8 April**, and dual-frequency L- and S-band signals followed on **16 April**, with the demonstration phase set to grow to **11** satellites.

u-blox is careful to frame LEO not as a replacement for Galileo, GPS, or BeiDou but as an added layer—one whose higher signal strength and fast-changing satellite geometry could speed up position convergence and improve robustness where signals are weak or contested. That framing matters because u-blox sits in phones, cars, and IoT modules, not just survey gear. Where the LEO-PNT conversation has so far centered on defense, timing, and high-end receivers, u-blox is the first to seriously ask whether the same signals belong in a consumer positioning stack—the point at which a new space layer stops being a specialist tool and starts being infrastructure everyone quietly uses.

### Iridium Closes Its Aireon Acquisition, Consolidating Space-Based Air Surveillance

**Iridium Communications** completed its acquisition of **Aireon**, the operator of the space-based **ADS-B** air-traffic surveillance system that rides on Iridium's satellite constellation, per Inside GNSS. Folding Aireon fully in-house gives Iridium direct ownership of a global aircraft-tracking service that air-navigation providers rely on to monitor flights over oceans and remote regions where ground radar can't reach. It's a smaller item than the LEO-PNT debate, but it points the same direction: space-based positioning, navigation, and surveillance services are consolidating into fewer, more vertically integrated hands as the strategic value of resilient overhead coverage keeps rising.
